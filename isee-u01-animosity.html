Animosity

/uh-NIM-uh-tee/ — rhymes with celebrity · noun

Hear It
What It Means

Animosity is a strong feeling of hostility or hatred toward someone.

Show it in context

She could barely hide her animosity toward her rival.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when animosity is the exact word.

Worked Example

Two families in the same town had feuded for generations, their animosity so deep that they refused to sit on the same side of the church.

Animosity is distinct from mere anger because it's a sustained, deep-rooted hostility between people in an ongoing relationship. It involves lasting opposition, not just a momentary feeling. The word captures the permanence and intensity of the hatred, making it more precise than anger or dislike.

Your Turn

My friend got mad at me when I was late to lunch, and she didn't speak to me for an hour.

Is animosity the right word here, or should it be something else?

Irritation

Correct — Irritation is a temporary reaction — exactly what a friend's short-lived cold shoulder is, not a permanent feeling.

Animosity

Not quite — Animosity is a deep, lasting hostility between people, not a brief moment of upset like a one-hour silence over a late lunch.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
dislike

Simple negative feeling. Animosity is far stronger and longer-lasting.

rancor

Bitter, long-lasting resentment. Similar to animosity but can be more about complaint than direct hostility.

malice

Ill will intended to cause harm. Malice is about desire to hurt; animosity is the hostility itself.

Antonyms · opposites

affection — Warm feelings of love and care — the opposite of animosity's deep hostility.

How Strong Is It?
dislike→bitterness→hostility→animosity

Animosity — the strongest: deep, lasting hostility between people. (our word)

Build the Word

animosus Latin: 'spirited, hot-tempered' (from animus, 'spirit, mind')

Animosity and animate both come from Latin animus, meaning 'mind' or 'spirit.' Animosity is when your spirit turns hostile against someone; animate is when you put life and spirit into something.

Though she had crushed her opponent in the election, she was magnanimous in victory, praising his effort and offering him a place on her team.

Same root, new word — what does magnanimous mean here?

very generous or forgiving, especially toward a rival or weaker person

Correct — You spotted the Latin root animus ('spirit, mind') shared by animosity and magnanimous. Add magnus ('great') and the root means 'great-spirited,' a winner whose spirit is large enough to praise the rival she just beat.

boastful and eager to celebrate a win loudly

Not quite — Loud boasting fits a victory scene, but it is the opposite of gracious generosity. The root animus means 'spirit, mind' and magnus means 'great,' so 'great-spirited' points to grace, not gloating.

extremely wealthy, owning many possessions

Not quite — The 'magn-' part can tempt you toward 'great wealth,' but the root here is animus ('spirit, mind'), not money. Magnanimous is about a large spirit, not a large fortune.

The root connection →

Animosity and magnanimous both grow from the Latin root animus, 'spirit, mind.' In animosity your spirit turns hostile against someone, while in magnanimous your spirit is so great (magnus) that you forgive or praise a rival instead of resenting them.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

neutral

Animosity goes deeper than anger or dislike. Use it when hostility is intense, long-lasting, and central to how two people relate. For a quick moment of anger, the plain word 'anger' or 'dislike' works better.
