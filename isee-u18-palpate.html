Palpate

PAL-payt — stress on first syllable, rhymes with allocate · verb

Hear It
What It Means

To palpate something is to examine it by touching or pressing on it with the hands, usually to check its condition.

Show it in context

The doctor palpated the swollen ankle to check for a break.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when palpate is the exact word.

Worked Example

The doctor pressed gently on Maria's abdomen with her fingers, feeling for any lumps or tender spots. This careful palpation revealed that her appendix was inflamed.

Palpate is the precise word for medical examination by touch. A plain word like 'pressed' or 'felt' would miss the specific, clinical intent—palpate means to examine by hand in a deliberate, diagnostic way.

Your Turn

Maria held the peach in her palm to check if it was ripe.

Did Maria palpate the peach, or is 'felt' the better word?

palpate

Not quite — Palpate is a clinical term for a careful medical examination by touch. Checking a peach for ripeness is far too ordinary for it, so it does not fit.

felt

Correct — Maria is just testing ripeness by touch, an everyday action. 'Felt' fits a simple touch, so it is the better word for checking a peach.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
press

To apply force with the hands, but it's a simple action. Palpate is pressing with medical or diagnostic purpose.

examine

To look at something carefully, but palpate specifically means examining through touch and hands.

manipulate

To handle or move something skillfully with hands, but palpate is specifically diagnostic touching, not general handling.

Antonyms · opposites

Build the Word

palpare Latin: 'to touch, to feel'

Palpate comes from a root meaning 'to feel or touch'—it's the careful, purposeful touching a doctor does when examining you.

She felt her palpitations increase as she climbed the stairs quickly.

Same root, new word — what does palpitation mean here?

a sharp pain in the legs from climbing

Not quite — Climbing stairs can make your legs ache, but palpitations are about the heartbeat, not leg pain, so this does not fit.

a feeling of being out of breath

Not quite — Being out of breath often happens alongside a racing heart, yet palpitation names the fast, pounding heartbeat itself, not the breathing.

a noticeable or rapid heartbeat, from the root meaning 'to feel or touch'

Correct — Palpate and palpitation come from the same Latin family: palpare, 'to touch or feel,' and its relative palpitare, 'to throb.' To palpate is to feel with the hands, and a palpitation is the heart felt throbbing in the chest.

The root connection →

The idea of touching runs through both words: a doctor palpates by pressing to feel the body, while a palpitation is the heartbeat you feel touching against your chest from the inside.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / medical

Palpate is a medical term for examining by touch. In casual contexts, use 'press,' 'feel,' or 'touch' instead. Choose palpate when discussing diagnosis or medical examination.
