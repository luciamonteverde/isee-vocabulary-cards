Deficient

/duh-FISH-ent/ — stress on FISH, rhymes with efficient · adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Deficient describes something or someone lacking a necessary quality or amount of something.

Show it in context

The inspector found the bridge deficient in three safety areas.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when deficient is the exact word.

Worked Example

The gym equipment inspection revealed deficient maintenance, with several machines showing rust and broken parts.

Deficient emphasizes a LACK of something necessary—here, proper maintenance. It is more precise than 'old' or 'broken' because it focuses on the failure to meet a required standard or condition.

Your Turn

After six months without practice, the pianist's skills were weak.

Is deficient the precise word here?

weak

Correct — After a break, the pianist's ability is simply reduced, which 'weak' captures. 'Deficient' would mean a required part of her skill was systematically missing, which is not the case here.

deficient

Not quite — 'Deficient' says something structurally necessary is lacking, like a diet deficient in vitamins. Rusty skills after time off are just diminished, so 'weak' is more precise.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
inadequate

Both mean 'not meeting standards,' but inadequate describes overall insufficiency; deficient pinpoints specific lacks or missing components.

incomplete

Both suggest something is missing, but incomplete means parts are absent; deficient means necessary qualities or amounts fall short.

substandard

Both mean below expected level, but substandard focuses on poor quality; deficient emphasizes systematic lack of required elements.

Antonyms · opposites

adequate — Sufficient and meeting requirements—the opposite of failing to provide what is necessary.

Deficient—lacking necessary qualities or amounts to meet required standards. (our word)

Build the Word

deficere Latin: 'to fail, to be lacking'

When you fall short of delivering what is needed, that failure creates deficiency—something essential is missing.

By the end of the year, the city was spending more than it earned and faced a large budget deficit.

Same root, new word — what does deficit mean here?

a surplus of extra money left over

Not quite — This is the reverse: a deficit means money is missing, not left over. The clue 'spending more than it earned' points to a shortfall.

a sudden one-time payment

Not quite — A deficit is an ongoing gap between what you have and what you need, not a single payment.

a shortfall, where the amount falls short of what is needed

Correct — Both come from Latin 'deficere,' to fail or fall short (de- plus facere, to do or make). Someone 'deficient' is missing what they need, and a 'deficit' is the amount by which money falls short.

The root connection →

A budget 'deficit' fails to cover its costs, just as a 'deficient' supply fails to meet a need. The 'deficere' root carries the idea of falling short in both.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal

Deficient is a formal word, especially common in official and technical contexts—use it when discussing failures to meet standards or requirements (safety inspections, medical assessments). For casual lack, simpler words like 'lacking' or 'inadequate' work better.
