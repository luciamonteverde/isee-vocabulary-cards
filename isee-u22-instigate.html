Instigate

IN-stih-gate / rhymes with navigate · verb

Hear It
What It Means

To instigate something is to be the one who starts or stirs it up, often trouble or conflict.

Show it in context

Investigators say a single email instigated the walkout.

Here's how to decide when instigate is the exact word.

Worked Example

After overhearing that the rival team had insulted their coach, one player instigated a full locker room confrontation by spreading the rumor before the coach could speak to calm everyone.

Instigate perfectly captures that this player is the originator—the one who deliberately STARTS the conflict. A word like trigger would just mean something caused a reaction, but instigate emphasizes being the trouble-STARTER who sets everything in motion.

Your Turn

The science teacher instigated the class experiment by explaining the first step.

Is instigate the right word here, or is something milder more fitting?

Instigated

Not quite — Instigate means to start or stir up conflict and trouble. A science teacher helpfully launching a class experiment is neutral and constructive, not troublemaking, so instigate carries the wrong tone here.

Prompted

Correct — This captures the teacher simply setting the activity in motion in a helpful, neutral way, without the sense of stirring up trouble that instigate implies.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
spark

To ignite or trigger a beginning; captures the 'starting' idea but doesn't emphasize deliberate troublemaking the way instigate does.

stir

To stir up or agitate; focuses on the stirring action itself rather than on being the deliberate originator of the trouble.

prompt

To cause or motivate someone; broader and more neutral than instigate, which specifically means to start trouble or conflict.

Antonyms · opposites

prevent — To stop something from happening; the opposite of instigate, which is to deliberately start or stir up trouble.

Build the Word

instigāre Latin: 'to incite or urge on'

Instigate originally meant to spur or drive someone forward — to push them deliberately into action, often troubling action.

A newborn foal has the instinct to stand within hours of birth.

Same root, new word — what does instinct mean here?

a natural inner urge to act, present from birth

Correct — Correct. 'Instinct' shares the 'prick, goad' root (Latin in- plus stinguere, a twin of stigare) with 'instigate': an instinct is an inner impulse that goads you into action.

a skill slowly learned through practice

Not quite — Not quite. An instinct is inborn, an urge that goads you to act without being taught, so it is the opposite of a skill built up through practice.

The root connection →

Both 'instigate' and 'instinct' trace back to a Latin root meaning 'to prick or goad.' To instigate is to goad others into action, while an instinct is an inner goad urging you to act.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

neutral / everyday

Use instigate when someone is the originator or deliberate starter of trouble or conflict. For milder stirring up of emotion, use stir or spark. For starting something neutral or positive, prompt or begin are clearer choices.
