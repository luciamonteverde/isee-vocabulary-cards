discouraged

dis-KUR-ijd

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Discouraged describes someone who has lost confidence in an effort they are making: after setbacks, they doubt it will succeed and feel less willing to keep going. The word looks ahead at the next attempt — a discouraged person has not quit yet, and can be talked into trying again.

See the word in context

After the third rejection letter she felt discouraged, but she mailed a fourth application anyway.

By the sixth week of guitar lessons, Marcus was discouraged: his fingers still stumbled over the same chord, and he had begun skipping practice.

Marcus is discouraged because week after week of failing at one chord has drained his confidence that the effort will pay off, and it shows in his will to keep going — he is skipping practice. Notice what has not happened: he has not quit the lessons, and one good week could bring him back. Confidence down, effort still alive — that is discouraged.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    dejected: Dejected describes the low, sunken state that settles over someone after one specific piece of bad news — head down, voice flat — and it looks back at what fell through. Discouraged looks forward: the question is whether the person still believes the effort can succeed and wants to keep at it. Ask whether the sentence is about how low the person feels after a piece of bad news or about whether they will keep trying; only the second is discouraged.

    frustrated: Frustrated means blocked from what you want and annoyed about it — the energy is still high, and it comes out as irritation, a slammed door, a sharp word. Discouraged is the opposite kind of energy: it drains away, and the person goes quiet and starts to doubt. Ask whether the person is worked up and annoyed or drained and doubtful; only the second is discouraged.

    demoralized: Demoralized is heavier and wider: confidence, hope, and discipline drain away together, usually across a whole team or workforce, until people stop believing that effort matters. Discouraged is the everyday, personal version — one person's confidence has taken a hit, but they are still in the game. Ask whether the sentence shows a group whose will to work has collapsed or one person doubting their next try; only the second is discouraged.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    encouraged: given fresh confidence to keep going

    hopeful: expecting the effort to succeed

    Where it sits · how much hope is left for the effort

    disappointed → discouraged → disheartened → despondent

    disappointed vs. discouraged: Disappointed is the first sting when one result falls short of what you hoped — a low grade, a rained-out game — and it is about that one result. Discouraged is what happens when the sting spreads to the future: the person starts to doubt the whole effort and feels less like continuing. Ask whether the feeling is about one result or about whether to keep going; only the second is discouraged.

    disheartened vs. discouraged: Disheartened goes further — the heart has gone out of the effort, and the person may stop for now. Discouraged is the lighter word: doubt has set in, but the person is usually still working, only with less confidence. Ask whether the person is still making the effort; if they have set the work down, disheartened fits better.

    despondent vs. discouraged: Despondent is the far end: hope is gone and the person has stopped trying altogether — they do not show up, do not send the next letter. Discouraged still has hope in it somewhere; a discouraged person can be talked into one more try. Ask whether the person has given up; if so, the word is despondent.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Discouraged is most often a feeling — the -ed form of the verb used as an adjective: a discouraged student, feeling discouraged. It is the plain, everyday word for the dip in confidence that setbacks bring, and it is usually temporary; a good coach or one small success can undo it. The same form is also the past tense of the verb: the vet discouraged us from feeding the dog table scraps, meaning she advised us against it — the act of putting someone off, which is the use your unit-mate deter sits beside.
