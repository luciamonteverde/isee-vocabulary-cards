ruin

ROO-in

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

To ruin something is to leave it past repair and past use. The thing may still be right there — a cake on the counter, a plan on paper, a good name — but nothing about it is worth saving anymore. Anything can do the ruining: a person, the weather, one small mistake. The word looks only at the result, and the result is total: not made worse, but finished.

See the word in context

One red shirt in the wash ruined every white shirt in the load, and no second wash would ever make them white again.

Water from a broken pipe ruined the old family photos; the pictures were still in their box, but every face had run together into gray.

The photos are ruined because they are past repair and past use: no one can bring the faces back, and a picture no one can see is no good as a picture. Notice what did NOT happen. The photos were not destroyed — they are still in the box, whole. And they were not merely damaged, because damage can be fixed and these cannot. Still there, past saving: that is ruin.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    spoil: To spoil something is to take the good out of it — the fun out of a party, the taste out of milk left too long, the surprise out of a gift — and the thing itself usually stays as it was: a spoiled evening still happened, a spoiled surprise still arrives. To ruin goes all the way: the thing is left past repair and past use. Ask whether what was lost was the pleasure or quality of the thing, or the thing itself.

    wreck: To wreck something is to smash or tear it apart by force — a car in a crash, a room in a fight, a ship on the rocks — so the harm is violent and you can see the pieces. To ruin needs no force and no pieces: a cake, a plan or a good name can be ruined with nothing broken at all. Ask whether something was broken apart by force, or simply left useless while still in one piece.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    repair: to fix something so it can be used again

    save: to keep something from being lost or made useless

    Where it sits · how much of the thing is left to save

    damage → ruin → destroy

    damage vs. ruin: To damage something is to harm it so that it works or looks worse than before, but a damaged thing can usually be fixed — a car with a broken light still drives, a torn page can be fixed with tape. A ruined thing is past that point. Ask whether the thing could be fixed and used again; if yes, it was damaged, not ruined.

    destroy vs. ruin: To destroy something is to break it apart or wipe it out so that it is gone — at most a pile of pieces is left. A ruined thing is often still there, whole and in one piece, just no good to anyone: the cake sits on the counter, the report is still on the screen. Ask whether the thing itself is gone, or still there but past use.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Ruin is an everyday word with a hard edge: it means the loss is total for that thing, so save it for cases where nothing is left to fix. It works for objects (rain ruined the shoes), for plans and events (one late bus ruined the whole trip), and for things you cannot touch at all (a lie ruined his good name). Ruin can also name a thing rather than an action: a house can fall into ruin, and tourists walk through the ruins of a castle — the same idea, what is left when something is past saving. This card teaches ruin as something that is DONE to a thing. Its unit-mate devastating describes how big a blow is and how much it hits; ruin describes what a blow, large or small, does to one thing. A tiny mistake can ruin a report without being devastating to anyone.
