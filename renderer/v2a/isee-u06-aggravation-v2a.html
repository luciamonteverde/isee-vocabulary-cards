aggravation

ag-ruh-VAY-shun

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Aggravation is the wearing, patience-eating bother of dealing with trouble that piles up while you are trying to get something done — the delays, the extra steps, the things that go wrong one after another. The trouble slows you and gets on your nerves, but it does not usually stop you; it is the kind of trouble that makes people mutter, 'I do not need this today.'

See the word in context

After the second detour, a road crew that waved him back the way he had come, and a parking meter that ate his coins, Mr. Delgado decided the concert was not worth the aggravation.

Renewing the family's passports took four trips to the post office, two rejected photos, and a form that had to be mailed twice, and by the end Aunt Rosa said the aggravation had cost her more than the fee did.

Nothing terrible happened to Aunt Rosa, and she got the passports in the end. What wore her down was the pile-up of small trouble on the way — trip after trip, photo after photo, a form mailed twice — while she was trying to get one ordinary task done. That worn-down, 'why is this so hard' bother is aggravation. If the office had refused the passports outright, she would have been stopped, and the sharper word would be frustration; if one photo had been rejected once and that was the end of it, it would have been a moment's irritation and nothing more.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    vexation: Vexation (also taught in this unit) is the word the ISEE pairs with aggravation, and the two are close. Vexation is the stewing, worked-up state of being bothered by something you cannot settle or shake off — a puzzle with no answer, a stain that will not lift — and it needs no task or errand at all; it also sounds older and a shade more worried. Aggravation comes from hassle on the way to getting something done. Ask whether the person is being put through trouble while trying to do something, or stewing over a thing that will not be settled.

    irritation: Irritation is the basic scratchy feeling that something is bothering you — one buzzing fly, one slow website — and it can flare and fade in a minute with no pile-up behind it. Aggravation is heavier and takes time to build: trouble after trouble while you are trying to get something done. Ask whether one small thing bothered the person once, or whether a string of trouble has been wearing them down.

    frustration: Frustration (from this unit's frustrate) is what you feel when you are blocked from something you want — the door will not open, the answer will not come, the goal is out of reach. Aggravation does not need the goal to be blocked: you will probably get there, but the trip is full of delays and things going wrong. Ask whether the person has been stopped from what they wanted, or merely put through hassle on the way to it.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    relief: the easing feeling when trouble is over or never comes

    contentment: the settled, satisfied feeling that nothing needs fixing

    Where it sits · how badly trouble has worn on a person's patience

    annoyance → aggravation → exasperation

    annoyance vs. aggravation: Annoyance is the plain, all-purpose word for being bothered, and it is usually mild and brief — a squeaky door, a friend who is five minutes late — with no real trouble behind it. Aggravation is annoyance that has been fed by real hassle: delays, extra steps, things going wrong while you were trying to get something done. Ask whether the person was simply bothered, or worn down by trouble.

    exasperation vs. aggravation: Exasperation is the point where patience runs out — the person throws up their hands, snaps, or gives up: 'That is it, I am done.' Aggravation is still short of that: the person is worn and muttering but is still dealing with the trouble. Ask whether the person is still pushing through, or has reached the end of their patience.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Aggravation is an everyday word, a little informal, and it usually names the feeling — 'I do not need the aggravation' — though it can also name the thing that causes it: 'that printer is a constant aggravation.' Watch for its other meaning: in a doctor's or a lawyer's mouth, aggravation means making something worse, as in 'aggravation of an old injury,' because the verb aggravate means to make worse. On the ISEE, the answer choice closest to aggravation is usually vexation — another word from this unit — and both are kinds of annoyance; what this card teaches is where the feeling comes from.
