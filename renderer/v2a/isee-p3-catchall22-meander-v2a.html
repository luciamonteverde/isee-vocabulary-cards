meander

mee-AN-der

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

To meander is to follow a slow, winding course — bending one way, then the other, in lazy curves — with no straight line and no hurry to get anywhere in particular. Rivers meander across flat valleys, country roads meander between farms, and a person with a free afternoon can meander through a town.

See the word in context

Below the old mill, the creek meanders across the meadow in wide bends, so a walk along its bank takes three times longer than the direct path home.

With nowhere to be until six, Teo meandered through the flea market, curving from a crate of old records to a rack of coats and back again.

Teo is not headed anywhere and is not in a hurry; his route bends from one stall to the next and doubles back, the way a slow river bends across a valley. A slow, curving course taken in no hurry, with no goal at the end, is exactly what meander describes — the word is about the shape of the path as much as the pace.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    wander: To wander is to move about with no fixed destination — you can wander straight down a road, wander fast, or wander lost through a strange city; the word says only that there is no set place to go. To meander says how the path itself looks: it curves lazily back and forth, and the pace is slow. Ask whether the sentence describes an aimless mover in general (wander) or a slow, bending course (meander).

    roam: To roam is to travel freely over a wide area — herds roam the plains, wolves roam for miles, a reporter roams the whole city — and the word points at how much ground is covered, not at how the path bends. Meander points at the curving line and the slow pace, and covers little ground for the time it takes. Ask whether the sentence is about ranging over a wide territory (roam) or about a slow, winding line (meander).

    zigzag: To zigzag is to move in sharp turns, first one way and then the other at hard angles — a skier cutting down a slope, a startled rabbit darting across a field, often at speed. To meander is to bend in soft, lazy curves with no sharp corners and no hurry. Ask whether the turns are sharp angles (zigzag) or gentle loops (meander).

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    dash: to run somewhere fast and by the most direct route

    march: to walk with firm, purposeful steps straight toward a goal

    Where it sits · how far a route drifts from a direct, purposeful line

    stroll → ramble → meander

    stroll vs. meander: Stroll is the lower step: a slow, relaxed walk taken for pleasure, and the route can be perfectly straight — down a pier, along one block, across the park. Meander adds the winding: the path itself bends back and forth, and only a walker, road, or river that curves lazily can meander. Ask whether the sentence is about the relaxed pace of a walk (stroll) or about a course that keeps bending (meander).

    ramble vs. meander: Ramble is the middle step: to walk about with no fixed route, or to talk on and on without reaching the point — its own card in this course teaches it as 'to wander without a clear plan.' Meander goes further: the course is not just unplanned but visibly winding, curving one way and then the other, and it belongs to rivers and roads as well as to people. Ask whether the sentence only says the route or the talk has no plan (ramble) or shows it bending lazily back and forth (meander).

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Meander began as the name of a famously twisty river in what is now Turkey, and it still carries that picture: lazy curves, no rush. It fits streams, roads, paths, and people in no hurry, and it stretches naturally to talk — a speech or conversation that meanders drifts from topic to topic with no destination in view. Use it when the winding itself is the point. If someone is simply lost, or is hurrying somewhere by a roundabout route, meander is the wrong word.
