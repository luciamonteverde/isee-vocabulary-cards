germinate

JUR-muh-nayt

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Germinate means to begin life as a plant from a seed: the seed soaks up water, splits its coat, and pushes out its first tiny root, usually before anything shows above the soil. It names only that first step, not everything the plant does afterward.

See the word in context

The seed packet warned that the pepper seeds would not germinate until the soil warmed to seventy degrees, so Dad kept the tray on top of the refrigerator.

Scientists planted a two-thousand-year-old date seed found in an old jar, and against every expectation it germinated, pushing out a pale root just as a seed planted last spring would.

Nothing about the seed had changed for twenty centuries; then water, warmth, and soil reached it, and it did the one thing germinate names — it split open and sent out its first root. The sentence is about that first step only. Whether the young plant later grew tall, put out leaves, or flowered would call for other words; germinate covers just the start.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    develop: Develop means to change step by step into a fuller or more advanced form, and it fits anything — a city, a skill, a storm, a photograph — at any stage of the process. Germinate is only the first step and only for a seed or a spore. Ask whether the thing started as a seed and whether the sentence is about the very beginning.

    grow: Grow covers the whole span of getting bigger and older — a puppy grows, a company grows, a tree grows for a hundred years. Germinate is the single moment at the start when a seed cracks open and life begins. Ask whether the sentence is about the beginning only or about the long stretch that follows.

    hatch: Hatch is the matching word for eggs: a chick, a turtle, or a caterpillar breaks out of its shell and its life in the open begins. Germinate is the same kind of first step, but for a seed breaking out of its coat. Ask whether the new life came out of an egg or out of a seed.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    rot: break down and decay instead of starting to live

    wither: shrivel and dry up, losing life rather than beginning it

    Where it sits · how far along a plant's growth has gone

    germinate → sprout → bloom

    sprout vs. germinate: Sprout is the next step — the green shoot pushes up into view above the soil, and you can point at it. Germinate happens just before that, when the seed splits and its first root appears, mostly out of sight. Sprout also fits things that were never seeds: a potato sprouts eyes, a cut stump sprouts new branches. Ask whether the sentence is about a seed's first stirring or about a shoot you can see.

    bloom vs. germinate: Bloom is much further along — the plant is fully grown and its flowers open. Germinate is the opposite end of the story, the day the seed first splits. Ask whether the sentence is about flowers opening or about a seed getting started.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Germinate is a beginning word, and a small one: it belongs to seeds and spores, to the days when a root first appears and little else is visible. Gardeners and scientists use it precisely. It also works for ideas and plans — a plan can germinate in someone's mind — but even then it means the first stirring of something that may later become large, not the finished thing.
