What It Means

Behest means an urgent request from someone in authority, weighty enough to set other people in motion.

See the word in context

At the mayor's behest, the city council moved its meeting to the larger auditorium so more residents could attend.

At the director's behest, delivered in a single phone call, the entire film crew assembles on set by the next morning, two weeks ahead of schedule.

One call from someone with real authority, and everyone moves. Behest almost always appears in the fixed phrase 'at someone's behest,' marking a request weighty enough to set other people in motion.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    directive: A directive is an official written instruction issued from the top of an organization -- a ministry circular, a head-office memo, numbered and filed. A behest is more personal and more urgent: one powerful person asks, and people move. A directive binds on paper; a behest moves people at a word.

    summons: A summons calls one particular person to appear -- before a court, a committee, or the headmaster -- and answer for something. A behest can ask for any action at all, and it flows from a person's own weight and authority rather than from a legal document.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    suggestion: an idea offered for consideration, not an order

    entreaty: a humble, earnest plea from a position of weakness

    Where it sits · how much force the request carries

    bidding → behest → command

    bidding vs. behest: Bidding is softer and more old-fashioned than behest -- doing someone's bidding may follow a mild request, while a behest carries urgency and the full weight of the requester's authority.

    command vs. behest: Command is even more forceful than behest: a behest is an urgent request that leans on authority and respect, while a command is a direct order that expects obedience with no discussion at all.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Behest lives almost entirely inside one fixed phrase: 'at someone's behest' -- at the king's behest, at her behest, at the committee's behest. If you find yourself writing behest without 'at' and a possessive in front of it, rework the sentence; the collocation is the word.
