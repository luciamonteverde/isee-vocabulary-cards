What It Means

Truism is a statement that is so obviously true that saying it adds little new information.

See the word in context

The coach's favorite truism -- 'practice makes perfect' -- didn't feel very useful the week his team lost every single game.

Nobody argues with the truism that the sun rises in the east; it's true, but it's hardly worth stating out loud.

A truism isn't wrong -- it's just so obviously correct that repeating it rarely teaches anyone anything new.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    platitude: A platitude is a worn-out saying delivered as if it were deep wisdom — 'everything happens for a reason,' offered with a meaningful nod. The pretense of depth is the point. A truism claims no depth at all: it is simply a statement so plainly true that saying it adds nothing.

    commonplace: A commonplace is an ordinary, unoriginal remark — the small talk of ideas, filler that neither claims wisdom nor promises truth. A truism is narrower: what defines it is being so obviously true that nobody could disagree, which is exactly why repeating it teaches nothing.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    revelation: a surprising, newly revealed truth

    insight: a fresh, deep understanding of something

    Where it sits · how firmly accepted as fact

    opinion → truism → postulate

    opinion vs. truism: An opinion is simply a personal view, which may or may not be true -- weaker than a truism, which is accepted as obviously correct.

    postulate vs. truism: A postulate is a statement formally assumed to be true as the foundation for an entire argument or theory, a more deliberate and structural claim than a casual truism.
