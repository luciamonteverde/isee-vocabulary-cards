renounce

rih-NOWNS

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

To renounce something is to give it up for good by saying so plainly, so that others know: of your own free will you declare that you no longer claim it, believe it, or will keep doing it — a title, a right, a belief, or a way of living — and you do not mean to take it back.

See the word in context

When he inherited the old mansion, Uncle Ray renounced his share so that the whole house could become the town library.

In a letter to the science journal, Dr. Okafor renounced the theory she had defended for most of her career, writing that the new evidence left her no honest choice.

She does not just quietly stop mentioning the theory or let it fade. She puts her giving-up in writing, where other scientists will read it, and says clearly that she no longer holds the idea — and there is no hint that she plans to pick it up again. A deliberate, open, final letting-go of something you held is what renounce names.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    abandon: To abandon is to leave something behind and walk away from it — a plan, a car, a half-finished project — often without a word and sometimes just by neglecting it. Renouncing is a spoken or written act: you announce that you are giving the thing up. Ask whether the person declared the giving-up or simply walked off.

    surrender: To surrender is to give something up because a stronger side has forced you to — an army surrenders a fort, a suspect surrenders a weapon. Renouncing is your own free decision, made without anyone taking the thing from you. Ask whether the person gave it up by choice or because they were beaten or made to.

    refuse: To refuse is to say no to something that is offered or asked of you — you turn it down before it was ever yours. You can only renounce what you already had or claimed. Ask whether the thing was already theirs: if they never held it, they refused it rather than renounced it.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    embrace: to take up a belief or cause gladly as your own

    keep: to hold on to what you have

    Where it sits · how formal and final the letting-go is

    drop → relinquish → renounce

    drop vs. renounce: To drop something is the casual, everyday way to stop — drop a class, drop a habit, drop a subject. There is no announcement, and you might pick it up again next month. Renouncing is formal and meant to be final: you state it openly and shut the door behind you. Ask whether the giving-up was casual and possibly temporary or declared and meant to last.

    relinquish vs. renounce: To relinquish is to let go of something you were holding — control, a seat, a right — often reluctantly and often by handing it to someone else, with no statement needed. Renouncing adds the open declaration: you say, for the record, that the thing is no longer yours. Ask whether the sentence shows only the letting-go or also the announcing of it.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Renounce is a formal word for serious, on-the-record giving-up: a prince renounces a throne, a new citizen renounces old loyalties, a writer renounces a view she once argued for. It fits things you held as your own — claims, rights, titles, beliefs, habits — rather than objects you can hand across a table. Because it says the decision is deliberate and meant to last, do not use it for everyday quitting: you renounce violence, but you just quit a club you got bored of. It usually carries a sense of principle, of choosing to let go for a reason.
