What It Means

A caveat is a specific warning, or a condition carrying a note of warning, attached to an offer, plan, or claim.

See the word in context

The lease looked generous, but it came with one caveat: the landlord could raise the rent after a single year.

The scholarship letter congratulates her warmly, then adds one caveat in bold print: the award applies only if she maintains a B-plus average every semester.

The condition is specific and attached to the offer itself -- a 'but be aware' clause built right in. A caveat is a formal warning or stipulation that limits how far a promise, offer, or claim extends.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    stipulation: A stipulation is a requirement spelled out as part of a deal -- do this, or the agreement is off. A caveat is not a demand but an alert: it warns you about a limit or a danger in the thing being offered. Stipulations bind; caveats caution.

    disclaimer: A disclaimer steps away from responsibility -- 'the publisher is not liable for errors' -- and protects the person speaking. A caveat protects the listener instead: it flags a specific limit or risk so the reader is not caught off guard. One shields the maker; the other warns the buyer.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    guarantee: an unconditional promise

    assurance: a confident statement meant to remove doubt

    Where it sits · how urgently it flags trouble

    proviso → caveat → warning

    proviso vs. caveat: Proviso is drier and more neutral than caveat -- a proviso is simply a condition written into an agreement, while a caveat adds a note of warning: pay attention, or this could go wrong.

    warning vs. caveat: Warning is blunter and more urgent than caveat: a caveat is a measured condition attached to an offer or claim, while a warning points straight at danger and demands immediate attention.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Caveat appears in patterns like 'with the caveat that...' and 'one caveat:' -- and survives in the legal Latin phrase caveat emptor, 'let the buyer beware.' Keep it for specific, stated warnings, or conditions carrying a note of warning, attached to an offer, plan, or claim -- not as a vague synonym for 'problem.'
