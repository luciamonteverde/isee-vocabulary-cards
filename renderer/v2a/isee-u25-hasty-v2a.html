hasty

HAY-stee

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Hasty describes an act or decision done so quickly that the speed itself cost something — a step got skipped, a detail got missed, or a conclusion was reached before the facts were in. A hasty thing is not simply fast; it was too fast to be done properly, and the result shows it.

See the word in context

In her hasty reading of the recipe, Nadia skipped the line about letting the dough rise, and the loaf came out as flat as a plate.

The mayor's hasty decision to cancel the harvest fair, made minutes after the first forecast and before anyone checked a second one, left a dozen food trucks parked in bright sunshine.

The decision was made in minutes, and the speed is exactly what went wrong: nobody checked a second forecast, so a fair was cancelled on a sunny day. A decision that was too quick to be made properly is hasty. If the mayor had waited a day, weighed two forecasts, and still cancelled, the choice might have been wrong, but it would not have been hasty — hasty always blames the speed.

    Accuracy

    “Does this word fit the meaning exactly, or does a neighbour fit better?”

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    rushed: Rushed says only that there was not enough time — a rushed lunch, a rushed goodbye — and the thing may still have turned out fine. Hasty adds a verdict: the speed cost care or thought, and something suffered for it. Every hasty act was rushed, so this rule has to do the work — if nothing went wrong, the word is rushed. Ask whether the sentence blames the speed for a mistake, a skipped step, or a bad call.

    careless: Careless means not paying enough attention, for any reason at all — boredom, tiredness, not caring — and a careless mistake can be made slowly, over a whole afternoon. Hasty blames one cause only: speed. Ask whether the sentence says the person was going too fast or simply not paying attention.

    impetuous: Impetuous describes a person's manner — sudden, hot, eager, swept along by a burst of feeling into charging ahead. Hasty describes the act or decision itself and says only that it was made too quickly to be done properly; a hasty glance at a map has no hot feeling in it. Ask whether the sentence is about a rush of feeling driving someone forward or about a task done too fast.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    deliberate: done slowly and on purpose, with each step thought through

    thorough: complete and careful, with nothing skipped

    Where it sits · how much harm the lack of thought does

    hurried → hasty → rash → reckless

    hurried vs. hasty: Hurried only reports that the clock was the problem — a hurried breakfast, a hurried note — and a hurried job can still come out perfectly well. Hasty is the next step up: the speed skipped a step or a thought, and the result shows it. Ask whether anything actually went wrong because of the speed.

    rash vs. hasty: Rash judges a decision by the danger it ignored — a rash promise, a rash bet, a rash dash across a busy road — and it is the risk, not the clock, that earns the word. Hasty judges by the time and thought that were skipped, and the damage may be as small as a missed detail. Ask whether the sentence is about a real risk being ignored or about a job done too fast.

    reckless vs. hasty: Reckless is the strongest word here: a reckless act ignores obvious danger to oneself or to others altogether — driving into a flooded road, skipping the safety harness. Hasty is far milder: the cost is usually a mistake or a missed step, not a threat to anyone's safety. Ask whether anyone could actually have been hurt.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Hasty is mildly critical — calling something hasty says it was done too fast to be done well. It attaches to acts and decisions (a hasty retreat, a hasty note, a hasty judgment) more often than to people, and its noun is haste: in his haste, he forgot the tickets. The form hastily turns up often on the ISEE. Keep hasty apart from premature, a word taught elsewhere in this course: something premature comes before the right time, however carefully it was prepared, while something hasty was prepared too fast, whenever it comes.
