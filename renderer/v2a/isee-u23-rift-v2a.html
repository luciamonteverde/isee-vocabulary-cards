Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

A rift is a break that opens up in something that used to be whole — a long split in rock or ground where one piece has come apart, or a falling-out that splits people who were once close — so that where there used to be one thing there are now two sides with an open space between them.

See the word in context

The band's rift began over who would sing lead and ended with the two singers refusing to be in the same room.

The climbers had to turn back when they reached a rift in the canyon floor too wide to step across and too deep to see the bottom of.

The canyon floor was once one continuous sheet of rock. Now it has split, and the split is wide enough to stop people — there is open air where solid ground used to be. A break that opens in something once whole, leaving two sides apart, is a rift, whether it is in stone or in a friendship.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    fissure: A fissure is a long, narrow, deep crack in rock, ice, or earth — narrow enough that you could step over it, though it may go down a long way — and it is the science word closest to a rift in the ground. A rift is wider, with the two sides pulled apart and open space between them, and rift also works for people while fissure never does. Ask whether the opening is a narrow line in a hard surface (fissure) or a real gap between two sides, or between two people (rift).

    gulf: A gulf is a very wide separation between two people, groups, or ways of thinking — so wide that they can hardly reach each other — and the two sides may never have been joined at all. A rift is a break: the thing was one piece and then split. Ask whether the two sides were once one thing that came apart (rift) or were simply always far apart (gulf).

    feud: A feud is a long-running fight between two sides — the arguing, insults, and payback that keep going for years. A rift is the break itself, the split that leaves people apart; it can be completely silent, with no fighting at all. Ask whether the sentence describes active fighting (feud) or the state of being split apart (rift).

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    bond: a close connection holding two sides together

    unity: the state of being one whole with no split in it

    Where it sits · how completely the once-whole thing has come apart

    crack → rift → rupture

    crack vs. rift: A crack is a thin broken line in a hard surface — a cracked cup, a cracked phone screen — and the object usually still holds together in one piece. A rift has gone further: the two sides have actually pulled apart and there is open space between them. Ask whether the thing is still one piece with a line through it or has split into two sides.

    rupture vs. rift: A rupture is a sudden, complete bursting apart — a pipe bursts, a friendship blows apart overnight — and it happens all at once. A rift can open slowly and may stand half-open for years without the two sides fully breaking off. Ask whether the break was a single violent burst (rupture) or a split that opened and remains (rift).

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Rift started as a word for a split in rock or ground — scientists still talk about rift valleys, where the earth's crust is slowly pulling apart — and that picture carries into its everyday use for people: a rift is a break in something that was joined. It is a fairly serious word: a rift between old friends, a rift inside a political party, a rift in a family. Use it when the two sides really were one unit before the break; for a plain disagreement that never split anyone, a smaller word will do.
