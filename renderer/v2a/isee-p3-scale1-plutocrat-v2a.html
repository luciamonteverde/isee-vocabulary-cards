plutocrat

PLOO-tuh-krat

Meaning

Hear It

What It Means

A plutocrat is a person who converts great wealth into political power; a member of a wealthy ruling class.

See the word in context

The documentary argued that a handful of plutocrats now controlled more political influence than most elected officials.

Historians describe the late 1800s in America as an age of plutocrats, when a few oil and railroad tycoons quietly shaped national policy.

A plutocrat's power doesn't come from a title or a vote -- it comes purely from having enormous amounts of money.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    tycoon: A tycoon is a hugely wealthy and powerful figure in business — someone who built or runs an industrial empire. A plutocrat is defined by what the money does next: it buys political power. A tycoon only becomes a plutocrat when the fortune starts steering governments.

    magnate: A magnate is a dominant, influential person within a particular industry — a shipping magnate, a steel magnate. The word measures standing inside business. Plutocrat measures something else entirely: rule by wealth, a private fortune converted into control over public power.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    pauper: a very poor person

    peon: a lowly worker with little status or power

    Where it sits · how much power the money buys

    wealthy → plutocrat → oligarch

    wealthy vs. plutocrat: Wealthy is far milder than plutocrat: it means having a lot of money and nothing more, while a plutocrat actively converts that money into political power.

    oligarch vs. plutocrat: An oligarch goes beyond even a plutocrat — one of a tiny circle holding nearly total political and economic control over an entire country, not just bought influence.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Plutocrat is almost always a critical word -- people rarely call themselves plutocrats. It's usually used to accuse someone of using money to buy influence they shouldn't have. (This unit's aristocrat, by contrast, holds power through birth and title, not necessarily wealth.)
