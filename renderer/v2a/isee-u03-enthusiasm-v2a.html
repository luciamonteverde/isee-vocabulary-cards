enthusiasm

en-THOO-zee-az-um

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Lively, glad liking for a thing itself — an activity, a subject, a plan, a team — that shows in how readily a person joins in and keeps coming back to it, whether or not anything special is happening right now. It is a feeling about the thing, not a reaction to an event, and it lasts past any single moment.

See the word in context

The new history teacher's enthusiasm for old maps was catching; by October half the class was trading copies of old maps with sea monsters drawn in the corners.

Mrs. Patel has played in the Tuesday bowling league for twenty years, and her enthusiasm still shows: she is first through the door, shoes already on, with a bag of snacks for the whole team.

Nothing special is happening — it is an ordinary Tuesday — yet Mrs. Patel's liking for bowling shows in what she does: arriving first, ready to play, bringing something for everyone. That is enthusiasm: a glad liking for the activity itself that keeps showing up week after week. If the feeling had been set off by one big night and faded afterward, the word would be excitement; if she were deeply devoted but never let it show, it would be closer to passion.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    excitement: a worked-up, can't-sit-still state set off by something that is happening or about to happen — a countdown, a surprise, a race — and it dies down once the moment has passed. Enthusiasm is a liking for a thing itself that keeps showing up. Ask whether the feeling would still be there on an ordinary day with nothing going on; if so, it is enthusiasm, not excitement.

    eagerness: a glad, restless wanting for something that has not arrived yet — the bus, the results, the first day. Enthusiasm needs no wait; it is a liking for the thing itself, whether or not anything is coming. Ask whether the sentence contains a wait for something ahead; if it does, the word is eagerness.

    passion: deep, strong feeling that can run quietly and privately for years, and can be serious rather than happy — a passion for justice needs no cheering. Enthusiasm is lighter and always shows. Ask whether the feeling shows in what the person does and sounds glad; a strong feeling kept private or kept grave is passion, not enthusiasm.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    apathy: no interest or feeling about the thing at all

    boredom: the thing has stopped holding any appeal

    Where it sits · how much a liking for something drives a person to take part

    interest → enthusiasm → zeal

    interest vs. enthusiasm: interest is mild attention — you listen, you read an article, you might ask a question. Enthusiasm is warmer and shows in joining in. Ask whether the person actually takes part or only pays attention; attention alone is interest.

    zeal vs. enthusiasm: zeal is enthusiasm turned into hard, steady effort toward a goal — it signs up, runs the meetings, and keeps working after everyone else has gone home. Enthusiasm is glad participation without that grind. Ask whether the sentence shows sustained hard work aimed at a goal; if it does, the word is zeal.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Enthusiasm is an everyday, warm word, usually followed by for (enthusiasm for chess) or about (enthusiasm about the plan), and its adjective is enthusiastic. It always names something visible: if you cannot point to what the person does — joining, signing up, showing up, talking about it — the word has been stretched. It is also always glad; a feeling set off by something frightening is not enthusiasm, however strong.
