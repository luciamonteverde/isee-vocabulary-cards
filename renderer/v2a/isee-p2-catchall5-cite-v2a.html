What It Means

To cite is to name the exact source of a claim, such as a page, section, or study, as evidence or support that others can check.

See the word in context

The lawyer was careful to cite the exact page number of the law she was relying on.

A journalist writes an article claiming a new law will raise taxes. To back up the claim, she cites the exact section of the law and the government website where readers can check it themselves.

She points directly to the exact source that supports her claim. Unlike 'mention,' which is casual, to cite specifically means naming the exact source as proof or evidence.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    quote: To quote is to repeat someone's exact words, word for word, usually inside quotation marks. To cite is different: citing points to the source, the page, the law, the study, as evidence, and you can cite something without repeating a single word of it.

    reference: To reference something is to mention it as a source of information, pointing readers toward it. To cite is the stricter, more formal act: in academic and legal writing, to cite means naming the exact source so a claim can be checked and verified.

    invoke: To invoke something is to call on it broadly as support for an argument: a speaker invokes tradition, a lawyer invokes a famous case. To cite is more precise, naming the exact source itself, like a page number, so others can check the evidence.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    omit: to leave out or fail to include

    ignore: to pay no attention to something

    Where it sits · how formally a claim is backed up

    mention → cite → document

    mention vs. cite: Mention is weaker than cite — mentioning something is casual, while citing means formally pointing to it as evidence or proof.

    document vs. cite: Document is stronger than cite in a sense: citing points to an existing source as support, but documenting means creating a full, detailed record of evidence yourself.
