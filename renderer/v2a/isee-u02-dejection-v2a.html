dejection

dih-JEK-shun

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Dejection is the low, deflated feeling that settles over you after a specific letdown — you tried for something, or counted on it, and it fell through. It shows: the shoulders slump, the voice goes flat, and for the rest of the day nothing seems worth the trouble. It lasts longer than the first sting of the bad news, but the person has not given up for good; by next week they may well try again.

See the word in context

After the bake sale brought in eleven dollars against the two hundred they had hoped for, the club sat at the bare table in dejection, nobody bothering to pack up the signs.

Marisol had rehearsed her speech for class president for two weeks; when the vote came in and she had lost by a landslide, she slid down in her chair, stared at her desk, and did not say another word until the bell.

Marisol is not in tears and not in despair — she will run for something again — but right now she has gone flat. She worked toward one specific thing, it fell through in front of everyone, and the energy has drained out of her: slumped in the chair, silent, staring. That heavy, deflated low after a letdown is dejection. If she had felt a quick pang and shrugged it off, the lighter word would be disappointment; if she had decided never to try for anything again, the heavier word would be despondency.

    Accuracy

    “Does this word fit the meaning exactly, or does a neighbour fit better?”

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    sadness: Sadness is the all-purpose word for feeling unhappy, for any reason or none, and it says nothing about how the person looks or what caused it. Dejection is one specific shape of sadness: it follows a letdown you can point to, and it shows in the body — slumped, quiet, drained. Ask whether there was one specific thing the person tried for or counted on that fell through.

    gloom: Gloom is a dark, heavy mood — or a dark atmosphere — that can hang over a person, a room, or a whole rainy week without any single cause; gloom is the weather, and dejection is the storm that just passed. Ask whether the low feeling followed one specific letdown or simply settled in.

    melancholy: Melancholy is a quiet, lingering, thoughtful sadness that needs no cause at all — it drifts in on a gray afternoon and can even feel a little sweet. Dejection is flatter and more sudden: something specific fell through, and the person has been knocked low by it. Ask whether the sadness arrived on its own or was caused by a particular letdown.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    elation: the high, soaring joy that follows a success

    cheerfulness: a bright, upbeat mood that lifts the people around it

    Where it sits · how low the spirits sink after a letdown and how much hope is left

    disappointment → dejection → despondency → despair

    disappointment vs. dejection: Disappointment is the first sting when a hope falls short — a sharp "oh" that can pass in minutes and leaves the person ready to move on. Dejection is what happens when that sting sinks in and stays: the person goes flat and heavy for the rest of the day or longer. Ask whether the feeling is a quick sting or a low that has settled in.

    despondency vs. dejection: Despondency goes further than dejection: the person has not only sunk low but has lost hope and stopped trying — they stop showing up, stop making the effort. Dejection still has hope in it somewhere; the dejected person will very likely try again. Ask whether the person has given up.

    despair vs. dejection: Despair is the bottom: hope is gone completely, and the person believes nothing can be fixed. Dejection is a low, not a bottom — heavy, but with the ground still under it. Ask whether the person believes things can still get better.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Dejection is the noun; the adjective dejected is more common in everyday speech — a dejected player, a dejected sigh. Both point at a low that you can see from across the room: slumped posture, flat voice, no energy. The word almost always follows a specific cause, and it usually passes in a day or two rather than settling in for good. Do not confuse it with plaintive, its neighbor in this unit: dejection is a mood a person is in, while plaintive describes a sad-sounding cry or voice, and a plaintive sound can come from a hungry animal that is in no mood at all.
