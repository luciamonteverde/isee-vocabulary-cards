stupefy

STOO-puh-fy

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

To stupefy someone is to hit their mind so hard that it stops working: they go blank, staring, unable to take in what is happening or to think of a single thing to say, and they stay that way for a while. Usually the cause is an overwhelming shock — news too big to absorb, a sight that should not be possible — though heat, thick smoke, or a sleepless night can stupefy a person in the same way. The mark of stupefy is that thinking has stopped, not just slowed, and that the blank lasts.

See the word in context

The curator's announcement that the museum's most famous painting had been a copy for forty years stupefied the visitors: nobody moved, nobody spoke, and one woman kept staring at the frame as if waiting for it to change.

Ten minutes of hail flattened the entire wheat crop, and the sight stupefied the farmer: he stood at the fence for an hour, hat in hand, not answering when his daughter called him in.

The farmer is not puzzled — there is nothing to figure out; the hail did what hail does. He is not merely surprised either, because a surprised man exclaims and asks questions. The size of the loss has knocked his mind flat: he cannot take it in, cannot think what to do next, does not even hear his daughter. A blank that lasts an hour is what stupefy names. Had he blinked, cursed, and started phoning the insurance company, he would have been stunned for a moment, not stupefied.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    astonish: Astonish is to surprise someone so much that they react with wonder — eyes wide, mouth open, 'I can't believe it!' — and the astonished mind is racing, full of questions. Stupefy stops the mind instead: the stupefied person has no questions and no words, only a blank stare, and the cause need not be a happy surprise at all — dreadful news or a flattened field can stupefy. Ask whether the person is bursting with amazed reaction, or has gone silent and blank.

    bewilder: Bewilder is to overwhelm someone with too much at once — gate changes, contradictory instructions — so that they lose their bearings and cannot make sense of what is going on, though they keep trying to. Stupefy needs nothing confusing: a single, perfectly clear piece of news can stupefy, and the stupefied person has stopped trying to make sense of anything. Ask whether the person is lost and still hunting for sense, or has understood exactly what happened and simply cannot think at all.

    numb: Numb is to take feeling away: a person who has gone numb feels nothing — no grief, no fear — yet may think and act perfectly well, signing forms and making calls in a flat, empty way. Stupefy takes thought away: the stupefied person may be flooded with feeling but cannot form a single clear idea or respond to anyone. Ask whether it is feeling that has switched off, or thinking.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    revive: to bring someone back to full awareness after a shock

    sharpen: to make someone alert and quick to react again

    Where it sits · how completely a shock knocks out the mind, and for how long

    daze → stun → stupefy

    daze vs. stupefy: Daze leaves someone foggy and slow in the head — after a fall, a long flight, or hard news — but still moving and functioning: a dazed person wanders around, answers slowly, gets on with things in slow motion. Stupefy goes further: thought stops outright, and the person can neither act nor respond. Ask whether the person is still functioning slowly in a fog, or has stopped altogether.

    stun vs. stupefy: Stun is a sharp shock that stops someone for an instant — a slap, a blinding flash, one line of bad news — and a stunned person comes back within a breath or two, blinking. Stupefy is the same stop stretched out: the blank lasts minutes or hours, and it can come from something slow, like heat or thick smoke, that never hits suddenly at all. Ask whether the person was halted for a moment by one sudden jolt, or left blank for a long stretch.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal / literary

    Stupefy is a strong, slightly old-fashioned word; in everyday speech people say 'stunned' or 'in shock.' You will meet it most in its adjective forms — a stupefied silence, a stupefying result — and in stupor, the noun for the dazed state itself. It works with any cause that shuts thinking down: an overwhelming shock is the usual one, but writers also say that heat, thick smoke, a long sleepless night, or a drug stupefied someone. Whatever the cause, the word promises the same thing: the person's mind has gone blank and stays blank for a while. Do not use it for a brief jolt that passes in a second, or for excitement that leaves someone talkative.
