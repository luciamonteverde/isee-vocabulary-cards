irritable

IR-ih-tuh-bul

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Irritable means easily annoyed — so easily that almost any small thing, a dripping tap or a question asked twice, draws a sharp, snapping reply. It is usually a passing state with a cause behind it, such as tiredness, hunger, pain, or worry.

See the word in context

After a twelve-hour shift, the nurse was so irritable that a patient's third question about visiting hours got a short, sharp answer she regretted at once.

Three days into a head cold, Dev was irritable: the hum of the refrigerator bothered him, his sister's humming bothered him more, and he snapped at her to stop.

Nothing big has happened to Dev. A refrigerator and a little humming would not bother anyone on a normal day, but the cold has left him with a hair trigger, so small things draw a snap. He is not furious, and he is not arguing with anyone; he is simply easily annoyed, and once the cold passes so will the mood. That is what irritable describes — a state, with a cause, that shows itself the moment some small thing sets it off.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    touchy: Touchy means quick to take offense about particular things — bring up a touchy person's weight, their grades, or their old best friend and they bristle, but everything else is fine. Irritable has no sore subject: when you are irritable, almost anything small can set you off. Ask whether one subject does the damage (touchy) or whether nothing in particular is needed (irritable).

    moody: Moody means given to sudden changes of mood, often down into gloom or silence — a moody teenager may shut the bedroom door and speak to no one. Irritable is one particular mood, the snapping one, and it always aims outward at whoever or whatever caused the annoyance. Ask whether the person's moods swing and sink (moody) or whether small things are drawing sharp replies (irritable).

    irascible: Irascible means quick to real anger — not sharp words but outbursts, a raised voice, a slammed door — and it describes a person's lasting temper rather than one bad day. Irritable stops at annoyance: a snapped reply, a sigh, a sharp look. Ask whether the reaction is a snap (irritable) or a blow-up (irascible).

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    relaxed: at ease and slow to be bothered by anything

    patient: able to put up with delays and small troubles calmly

    Where it sits · how deep and how lasting the bad temper runs

    grumpy → irritable → cantankerous

    grumpy vs. irritable: Grumpy is the lower step — a sour, complaining mood that shows on its own, in sighs and a long face, whether or not anything has happened. Irritable adds a hair trigger: the mood stays hidden until some small thing sets it off, and then it snaps at whoever is nearest. Ask whether the person is grumbling into their coffee with nothing to blame (grumpy) or snapping the moment someone asks a question (irritable).

    cantankerous vs. irritable: Cantankerous is the far step — a lifelong habit of grumbling, objecting, and arguing back, so that being difficult has become part of who the person is. Irritable is a state: a normally cheerful person can be irritable for one bad morning and be back to normal by lunch. Ask whether the sentence describes one bad stretch with a cause (irritable) or a whole character built around arguing (cantankerous).

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Irritable is the everyday, neutral word for a cross, snapping mood, and it is gentler than any word for real anger — you can say it about a baby, a tired friend, or yourself without insulting anyone. Doctors borrow it too: irritable skin or an irritable stomach is one that reacts to the slightest thing, the same idea of a hair trigger. The verb is irritate, and the adjective for the person on the receiving end is irritated.
