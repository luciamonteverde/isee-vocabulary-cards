Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Dread is the heavy, sinking feeling you carry when something bad is on its way and you are sure it is going to happen — the week before a surgery, the night before a punishment that has already been announced. It builds ahead of time, and the person is not hoping things might turn out fine; they expect the worst.

See the word in context

Ever since the letter arrived, a quiet dread had hung over the house: on Monday the bank would decide whether the family could keep the farm.

With every mile marker, Imani's dread grew heavier — at the end of this drive was the animal shelter, and the dog she could no longer keep.

Nothing has happened yet, but Imani is not wondering whether the day will go badly; she knows exactly what is waiting at the end of the road, and she knows it will be awful. That heavy, certain, sick-at-heart feeling about something still ahead is dread. If she were only nervous that the shelter might not have room, the lighter word would be apprehension.

    Accuracy

    “Does this word fit the meaning exactly, or does a neighbour fit better?”

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    fear: Fear is the big, general word for being scared, and it covers everything from a spider on your arm right now to an uneasy sense that the woods are not safe. Dread is one specific kind: fear of something still ahead that you are sure is coming. Ask whether the scary thing is already here or still on its way — and whether the person is certain it will arrive.

    worry: Worry is the thinking — turning a problem over and over in your mind — and it can be about anything at all: a sick pet today, a bill from last month, a test next week. Dread is a feeling in the stomach, not a thought, and it is aimed at one bad thing you know is coming. Ask whether the person is chewing on a problem or waiting, sick at heart, for something they know will happen.

    horror: Horror is the shock you feel when you see or learn about something awful — a car crash, a cruel act — so it always looks at something that has already happened or is happening in front of you. Dread looks ahead, at something that has not arrived yet. Ask whether the awful thing is already in view or still to come.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    anticipation: the eager, hopeful looking-forward to something good that is coming

    hope: the belief that what is coming will turn out well

    Where it sits · how heavy the fear is and how sure the person is that harm is coming

    apprehension → dread → terror

    apprehension vs. dread: Apprehension is the lighter word — an uneasy, nagging feeling about one coming event that might go badly, but might not: a test, a doctor's visit, a hard conversation. Dread is heavier and sure of itself: the person has stopped hoping and expects the worst. Ask whether the person thinks the thing might go wrong or knows it will.

    terror vs. dread: Terror is the heaviest word — fear so strong it takes over the whole body, and it usually hits when the danger is right in front of you, not next week. Dread is quieter and comes earlier: a weight that settles in while the bad thing is still on its way. Ask whether the danger has arrived or is still approaching.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Dread is a heavy word, and it points forward: you feel dread about, or at the thought of, something coming, and it is usually something you cannot get out of. It is also a verb — you can dread Mondays or dread the dentist — and the adjective dreaded describes the thing itself: the dreaded exam. People use it lightly for small things as a joke, but on the ISEE it names real, serious fear of what is coming.
