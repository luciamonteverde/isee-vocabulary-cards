extravagant

ik-STRAV-uh-gunt

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Extravagant means spending, giving, or using far more than the occasion calls for — more money, more show, more of everything than good sense allows — and the word always shakes its head at it, even when the person spending can well afford it.

See the word in context

Theo spent his entire summer paycheck on one pair of sneakers, an extravagant choice that left him borrowing bus fare by August.

For their two-year-old's birthday, the couple rented a carousel, hired a band, and set off fireworks over the lake — an extravagant party for a child who fell asleep before the cake.

A birthday for a toddler calls for a cake and a few friends. A carousel, a live band, and fireworks go so far past that — and the guest of honor slept through most of it — that the sentence cannot help shaking its head. That gap between what the occasion called for and what was spent, together with the disapproval, is what makes the party extravagant. Notice that the couple could plainly afford it all; extravagant judges the spending, not the bank account.

    Accuracy

    “Does this word fit the meaning exactly, or does a neighbour fit better?”

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    excessive: Excessive means too much of anything — noise, homework, salt — and it attaches to amounts and degrees, always with a frown. Extravagant is the spending specialist: it describes what was bought, given, or shown off, and the person doing it. Ask whether the sentence is about too much of some amount (excessive) or about someone spending or showing off beyond what sense allows (extravagant).

    luxurious: Luxurious describes the thing itself — soft, rich, comfortable, costly: a luxurious hotel room, a luxurious coat — and it passes no judgment on whoever paid. Extravagant judges the choice to pay: it says the spending went too far. Ask whether the sentence is describing how rich something feels (luxurious) or criticizing what someone spent on it (extravagant).

    wasteful: Wasteful means using up more than you need and getting nothing back — lights left burning, food scraped into the trash — with no glamour anywhere in it. Extravagant is waste with a shine on it: too much spent on show, luxury, or pleasure. Ask whether something is simply being used up and thrown away (wasteful) or spent on more than the occasion deserved (extravagant).

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    frugal: careful with money as a steady habit, spending only what is needed

    modest: simple and kept within sensible limits

    Where it sits · how far spending goes past what good sense allows

    lavish → extravagant → prodigal

    lavish vs. extravagant: Lavish is the lower step — rich, overflowing plenty poured out by a generous hand, and the word approves: a lavish breakfast, lavish praise. Extravagant is the same overflow once it has gone past good sense, and the word does not approve — it is counting the cost. Ask whether the sentence is pleased by the plenty (lavish) or shaking its head at it (extravagant).

    prodigal vs. extravagant: Prodigal is the far step — spending so wildly that the person pours out what they cannot afford to lose and ends up with nothing. Extravagant judges the spending, not the bank balance: a billionaire's extravagant yacht is safe money badly spent. Ask whether the sentence shows a fortune being used up (prodigal) or simply spending that went far past what was needed (extravagant).

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Extravagant almost always comes with a raised eyebrow, and it can attach to more than money: an extravagant compliment or an extravagant promise is one that goes far beyond what the moment deserved. It is judged against the occasion, not the wallet — a rich family's party can be extravagant even though they never notice the bill, and a poor student's single wild purchase can be extravagant too. On a test, watch for the shake of the head: if the sentence approves of the plenty, another word is wanted.
