Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Brackish describes water that has a little salt in it — more than a river or a rain barrel, far less than the open ocean. It is the water of tidal creeks, coastal marshes, and wells near the shore, and it is unpleasant to drink.

See the word in context

The old well behind the beach cottage gives brackish water — fine for rinsing sandy feet, but nobody could stand to drink it.

Where the river slows and meets the bay, its water turns brackish, and the fish that live there have to handle a little of the sea in every mouthful.

At that spot the fresh river water is mixing with salt water pushed in by the tide, so the result is neither one nor the other: it has more salt than the river upstream but far less than the bay beyond. That in-between amount of salt is exactly what brackish names. Farther up the river the water is fresh; farther out it is simply seawater, and neither of those is brackish.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    salty: Salty is the everyday word for anything that tastes of salt, at any strength — popcorn, seawater, a tear. Brackish is narrower in two ways: it is said only of water, and only when the salt is light, well short of the ocean's. Ask two things: is it water, and is it only somewhat salty?

    saline: Saline is the scientist's and doctor's word: it states that salt is present, at any strength, and it is usually said of liquids people make or measure — a saline drip in a hospital, saline solution for contact lenses, a saline lake with more salt in it than the sea. Brackish is said of natural water and always means only somewhat salty. Ask whether the writer is naming a measured or man-made solution or describing water found outdoors.

    murky: Murky means cloudy and dark, so you cannot see through it — mud or rotting leaves stirred into the water. It is about what floats in the water, not what is dissolved in it; a brackish creek can run perfectly clear, and a murky pond can be fresh. Ask whether the problem is that you cannot see through the water or that it tastes of salt.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    pure: clean water with nothing dissolved in it

    drinkable: safe and pleasant enough to swallow

    Where it sits · how much salt the water carries

    fresh → brackish → briny

    fresh vs. brackish: Fresh water carries no salt worth tasting — a mountain stream, a lake, rain in a barrel — and you can drink it. Brackish water has crossed the line: some salt has gotten in, enough to taste and enough to make you spit it out. Ask whether the water tastes of salt at all.

    briny vs. brackish: Briny is the stronger word — as salty as the sea or even more so, like the ocean itself or the liquid pickles float in. Brackish water is only partway there: it has picked up some salt but nowhere near a full sea's worth. Ask whether the water is as salty as the ocean or only a little salty.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Brackish is almost always said of water — and of the places that water makes, such as a brackish marsh or a brackish lagoon. It carries a wrinkled-nose feeling: brackish water tastes wrong and cannot be drunk, even though it is nowhere near as salty as the ocean. Sailors, campers, and anyone with a well near the coast use it as a warning. Do not use it for food; an over-salted soup is never brackish.
