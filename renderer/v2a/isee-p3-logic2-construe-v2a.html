Meaning

Hear It

What It Means

To construe words or actions is to settle on what they were meant to do — to pin one meaning onto them, usually in the form 'construe X as Y' — when the words themselves are in plain sight and open to more than one way of being taken.

See the word in context

The school handbook allowed students to bring 'a small snack,' and one teacher construed that as a granola bar while the teacher next door construed it as a whole bag of chips.

The treaty promised that the river would stay 'open to all vessels,' and for fifty years each country construed those three words in whatever way suited its own fishing fleet.

The three words are not hidden, faded or in code; both countries can read them perfectly well. What they cannot agree on is what the words were meant to do — whether 'all vessels' covers fishing boats, and whose fishing boats — so each side pins the meaning that helps it. Picking one meaning for plain words that allow more than one is exactly what construe names, and the fact that the two readings clash for fifty years shows why the word so often turns up in disputes.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    interpret: Interpret is the wide, everyday word for working out what anything means — a poem, a chart, a dream, a shrug — and it fits wherever construe fits. Construe is the narrower, more careful word: someone settles on what particular words or actions were meant to do, usually in the form 'construe X as Y,' and often where the reading could be argued with. Ask whether the sentence is about making sense of something in general (interpret) or about pinning one meaning onto specific words or acts that could be taken more than one way (construe).

    decipher: To decipher is to make out something hard to read or make sense of — faded handwriting, a smudged label, a worn old inscription. The struggle is with the marks themselves. Construe begins after that struggle is over: the words are clear and easy to read, and the question is what they were meant to do. Ask whether the trouble is making out what the words are (decipher) or deciding what plainly written words mean (construe).

    parse: To parse a sentence is to take it apart — find the subject, the verb, and how the pieces connect — and the job ends at structure. Construe goes one step further, to intent: what the words, once taken apart, were meant to do. Ask whether the sentence is about the grammar and parts of a sentence (parse) or about the meaning someone takes from it (construe).

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    misconstrue: to take words or actions the wrong way, settling on a meaning they were never meant to carry

    overlook: to pass over words or actions without taking any meaning from them at all

    Where it sits · how much the meaning resists the reader before it gives itself up

    understand → construe → decode

    understand vs. construe: Understand is the lower step — the meaning is plain, you take it in, and nobody argues about it. Construe is what happens when the words could be taken more than one way and someone decides which way: a construal can be disputed, and often is. Ask whether someone could reasonably disagree about the meaning taken (construe) or whether the words simply say what they say (understand).

    decode vs. construe: Decode is the far step — the meaning was hidden on purpose, in a cipher, a scrambled signal or a secret code, and it has to be unlocked with the right key or system. Construe deals with words that were never hidden at all: they sit in plain sight, and the work is choosing what they were meant to do. Ask whether the meaning was locked away on purpose (decode) or open but able to be taken more than one way (construe).

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Construe is a careful, slightly formal word, at home in courtrooms, contracts, school rules and arguments about what someone 'really meant.' It nearly always comes with a reading attached — you construe a remark as an insult, a clause as a promise, a silence as a yes — and it quietly hints that another reading was possible. On the test the answer it pairs with is interpret; its wrong-way twin is misconstrue, a word from earlier in this course, which is construing something the wrong way.
