Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

A person whose mind works so slowly that ideas others grasp at once must be explained to them again and again, and who shows no spark of curiosity about anything.

See the word in context

The villain in the play keeps a dullard for a servant — a man who has to be told three times where the door is and never once wonders why his master goes out at midnight.

In the Sunday cartoon, the hero's sidekick is a cheerful dullard who needs the rules of checkers explained before every single game.

Checkers has a handful of rules, and most people keep them after one game. The sidekick has heard them dozens of times and still needs them again, and he never gets curious enough to work them out for himself. A mind that slow to take things in, with no spark behind it, is what dullard names — he is not wicked, and he has not done anything foolish; he is simply slow.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    dunce: a student who does badly at lessons — the word belongs to the classroom, and it judges the schoolwork, not the mind behind it; a lazy but quick student can be called a dunce. Dullard judges how slowly a person takes anything in, anywhere. To tell them apart, ask whether the sentence is about failing at lessons or about a slow mind.

    simpleton: a person who believes whatever they are told and is easily tricked, because they lack ordinary common sense. The simpleton's trouble is trust, not speed. To tell them apart, ask whether someone got fooled.

    ignoramus: a person who knows very little and often talks loudly anyway; the problem is missing knowledge, and a quick mind can be an ignoramus about a subject it never studied. Dullard is about the speed of the mind itself. To tell them apart, ask whether the trouble is what the person does not know or how slowly they think.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    genius: a person of rare, exceptional intelligence.

    prodigy: a young person whose ability already runs years ahead of their age.

    Where it sits · how deeply the word faults a person's mind

    fool → dullard → dolt

    fool vs. dullard: almost anyone can be called a fool for one silly act — the wrong bet, the rash promise — and a quick, clever person can be a fool on Tuesday. Dullard is a judgment on the mind's whole pace, not on one act. Ask whether the sentence points at a single foolish thing someone did or at how slowly the person takes everything in.

    dolt vs. dullard: both name a slow-witted person, a beat behind everyone else, but dolt is the blunt, heated word — what you call someone to their face after they have missed the obvious. Dullard is cooler and more bookish, the label a writer pins on a character. Ask whether the speaker is throwing an insult in the heat of the moment or coolly describing what a person is like.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    old-fashioned / insulting

    Dullard is an old-fashioned insult that lives mostly in novels, plays and satire; in real speech people say slow or dim. It judges two things at once — the pace of the mind and the lack of any spark or curiosity — and nothing else: a dullard may know plenty of facts and may never do anything foolish. It is a noun for a person, never a describing word for a boring lecture or a blunt knife. Because it is a label for someone's whole mind, it is far too unkind to use about a real classmate; keep it for characters.
