excitement

ik-SYT-munt

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

A worked-up, can't-sit-still state — heart beating faster, voice rising, feet moving — set off by something that is happening or is about to happen, and that dies down once the moment has passed. It is a reaction to an event, not a liking for a thing, and it can come with a happy happening or a scary one.

See the word in context

By the time the launch count reached ten seconds, the excitement in the viewing area had every visitor on their feet, phones raised and voices rising with each number.

Children pressed against the shop window in excitement as the baker pulled the first batch of holiday cookies from the oven.

The feeling here has a trigger — the batch coming out right now — and it shows in bodies that cannot hold still, pressed to the glass. Once the cookies are bought and eaten, the feeling dies down. A reaction to something happening, which goes away when the moment passes, is excitement. If the children simply loved baking and showed up at the shop every week, the word would be enthusiasm; if they were quietly looking forward to a batch due next week, it would be anticipation.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    enthusiasm: a glad liking for a thing itself — a hobby, a subject, a team — that shows in joining in and keeps showing up on ordinary days. Excitement needs a happening to set it off and goes away when the happening ends. Ask whether the feeling would still be there on an ordinary day with nothing going on; if so, it is enthusiasm, not excitement.

    anticipation: looking ahead to something you know is coming — a test, a party, a storm — and you can do it quietly, or even while afraid of it. Excitement is the worked-up state itself, whether the thing is coming or already here. Ask whether the person is worked up and unable to sit still; quiet looking-ahead is anticipation, not excitement.

    thrill: one sharp, sudden rush of feeling — the drop of a roller coaster, a last-second goal — over almost as soon as it starts. Excitement can build for a whole afternoon and last through an event. Ask whether the feeling is a single quick rush or a worked-up state that lasts; a quick rush is a thrill.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    boredom: nothing that is happening holds any pull

    calm: settled and still, nothing going on

    Where it sits · how worked up a person is by what is happening

    interest → excitement → frenzy

    interest vs. excitement: interest is mild, steady attention — you follow along, you ask a question. Excitement is that attention set racing by something happening. Ask whether the person can sit still and speak in a normal voice; if they can, it is interest.

    frenzy vs. excitement: frenzy is excitement that has gone over the edge into wild, out-of-control activity — people pushing, screaming, past reason. Excitement keeps its head. Ask whether anyone has lost control; if the crowd is out of control, the word is frenzy.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Excitement is the everyday word for being worked up by what is happening, and it does not care about the cause: the excitement of a birthday, of a big game, or of a fire alarm going off. Its verb is excite and its adjective excited. The thing to hold onto is that it comes and goes with the event — once the moment is over, so is the excitement. A liking that lasts through ordinary days has a different name.
