Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Inert is lacking any power inside to move or react at all.

See the word in context

The old engine sat inert in the barn, untouched and unmoving for thirty years.

After the power cut, the whole factory floor goes silent and inert: conveyor belts frozen mid-load, robot arms stopped in place, not one moving part anywhere in the building.

Nothing has the power to move at all -- that is the word's plainest sense. Inert also describes living things that will not stir (an inert body on a stretcher) and substances that will not react (the inert gases in a chemistry lab).

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    immobile: Immobile means unable to move or to be moved -- a boulder wedged in a canyon is immobile. Inert points at what is missing inside: no power, no life, no reaction. An inert engine can still be towed away; an immobile one cannot even be shifted.

    unresponsive: Unresponsive means giving no reaction when something tries to rouse it -- a patient who does not answer, a screen that ignores every tap. Inert goes deeper: an inert thing has no power to move or react at all, not merely no answer at this moment.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    active: moving and doing things

    mobile: able to move freely

    Where it sits · how deep the stillness goes

    motionless → inert → lifeless

    motionless vs. inert: Motionless notices only that nothing is moving right now -- a motionless deer is frozen but ready to bolt -- while an inert object has no power to move at all.

    lifeless vs. inert: Lifeless goes past inert into something bleaker: an inert body may simply be unable to move, while a lifeless one shows no sign of life whatsoever.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Inert works in three registers: motionless objects (an inert hulk of a car), unresponsive bodies (the patient lay inert), and non-reacting substances (helium and neon are inert gases). Its noun inertia has escaped the science lab entirely and now describes any inability to get started.
