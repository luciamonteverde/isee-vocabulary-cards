fictitious

fik-TISH-us

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Fictitious describes something that was invented and does not exist — a person, a company, a name, an event — but that has been given the outward shape of a real one, with a name, a photo, an address, or a date, so that it can be taken for real. Nothing genuine stands behind it: someone made it up from nothing and dressed it as real.

See the word in context

The tiny town of Agloe on the old road map was fictitious: the people who drew the map had added it, dot and label and all, so they could tell if a rival traced their work.

For years the class newspaper ran an interview with a fictitious student, 'Bartholomew Quince,' whose invented opinions somehow always agreed with the editor's.

Bartholomew Quince has a name, a grade, and quoted opinions — everything a real student would have on the page — and readers take him for one. But no such person exists; the editor made him up from nothing. That is what makes him fictitious rather than simply false: a false statement is a wrong claim about real things, while a fictitious student is an invented thing dressed to pass as real.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    imaginary: Imaginary describes something that exists only in someone's mind — an imaginary friend, an imaginary kingdom in a game — and nobody dresses it up to fool anyone; it is openly not real. Fictitious things are given the outward shape of real ones, with names, dates, and paperwork, so they can pass as real. Ask whether the invented thing is being passed off as part of the real world.

    fake: Fake is the everyday word for anything that is not the genuine article, and a fake thing usually exists — a fake watch is a real watch pretending to be a famous brand. A fictitious thing does not exist at all: there is no company, no person, only an invented name. Ask whether there is a real object imitating a genuine one, or nothing there but an invention.

    forged: Forged means copied or altered to pass as genuine — a forged signature copies a real person's handwriting, a forged ticket copies a real one. Forging needs a genuine original to copy. Fictitious needs none: the person or company never existed anywhere. Ask whether a real original was copied, or whether the thing was invented from nothing.

    fabricated: Fabricated points at the work of building a lie — someone put together a false story or false evidence piece by piece, on purpose, to deceive. Fictitious describes only what the thing is: invented and not real. A fabricated alibi is one someone constructed in detail; a fictitious address is simply one that does not exist. Ask whether the sentence is about the building of the lie, or only about the thing not being real.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    genuine: real, and exactly what it claims to be

    actual: existing in fact, not invented

    Where it sits · how far the untruth goes toward cheating someone

    false → fictitious → fraudulent

    false vs. fictitious: False is the plain, wide word — anything not true is false, including an honest mistake: a false answer on a quiz, a false date copied wrong onto a form. Fictitious is narrower: the thing itself was invented and never existed, and it wears the shape of something real. Ask whether the sentence is about a wrong claim about real things, or about an invented thing that was never there at all.

    fraudulent vs. fictitious: Fraudulent goes further: the fake is being used to cheat someone out of money, property, or rights, which is a crime. A fictitious company is one that does not exist; a fraudulent company is one used to take people's money. Ask whether someone is actually being cheated, or whether the thing is simply not real.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Fictitious is a formal word and usually a suspicious one: a fictitious name on a hotel register, a fictitious company on an invoice, a fictitious witness in a news story. You will also hear it used for people and places in stories, but on the ISEE it pairs with forged and fake — the point is that the thing was made up and is being passed off as real. It describes the thing itself, not the person who made it up; for the maker's act of building the lie, English uses fabricate.
