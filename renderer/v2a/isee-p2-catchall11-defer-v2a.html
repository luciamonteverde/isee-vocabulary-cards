What It Means

To defer is to yield to another person's judgment or authority on a particular matter, out of respect.

See the word in context

On questions of courtroom procedure, even senior lawyers defer to the judge's authority without argument.

Two head chefs disagree about how to plate a dish, but when the restaurant's founder walks in and gives her opinion, both chefs immediately defer to her judgment.

That willingness to yield out of respect for greater authority is exactly why defer fits here. Unlike 'ignore,' which dismisses another's opinion, defer respects and follows it.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    submit: To submit is to place yourself under another's control or authority, often completely and for good. To defer is narrower and lighter: you accept someone's judgment on one particular question, out of respect, while remaining your own person. Submission hands over control; deferring hands over one decision.

    comply: To comply is to follow a rule, order, or request, whatever you privately think of it. To defer is different: you accept a person's judgment because you respect their knowledge or standing, not because a rule or order requires it.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    override: to use one's authority to reject another's decision

    overrule: to reject a decision using greater authority

    Where it sits · why you go along with it

    yield → defer → acquiesce

    yield vs. defer: Yield is a bit more general than defer -- yield can describe giving way in almost any contest or pressure, while defer specifically implies respecting someone's greater knowledge or authority.

    acquiesce vs. defer: Acquiesce can feel more reluctant than defer: defer often shows genuine respect for someone's judgment, while acquiesce can describe going along with something only because resisting seems pointless.
