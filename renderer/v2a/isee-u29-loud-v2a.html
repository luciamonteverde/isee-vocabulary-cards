Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Loud describes a sound — a voice, a bang, a piece of music — that carries so much volume it reaches across the whole space, so that everyone there can hear it whether they want to or not.

See the word in context

The music from the apartment above us was so loud that the pictures on our wall began to shake.

The fire alarm in the school kitchen was so loud that students in the gym, three rooms away, covered their ears.

The sentence never says whether the alarm was rough or pleasant, only that its sound reached three rooms away and made people cover their ears. That reach — the volume — is what loud measures. A quiet tone in the same kitchen would not be loud, however annoying it was, and a bell that could be heard only by someone standing right beside it would not be loud either.

    Section 2

    Accuracy

    “Does this word fit the meaning exactly, or does a neighbour fit better?”

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    harsh: Harsh describes a sound that is rough and painful on the ear — metal on metal, a voice worn rough by a cold — and a sound can be harsh at any volume; a quiet harsh sound is still harsh. Loud measures only how much sound there is, and a loud sound can be lovely, like a full band or a crowd singing. To tell them apart, ask whether the sentence is about how much sound reaches the ear or about how rough that sound is.

    clear: Clear means easy to make out — every word can be understood, every note is distinct — and a clear voice can be quite quiet. Loud is about volume alone: a voice can be loud and still be a wall of sound that no one can follow. Ask whether the sentence is about how much sound reaches the ear or about how easily the words or notes can be made out.

    vocal: Vocal, another word in this unit, describes a person who keeps saying what they think, again and again, until it is heard — and that can be done in a calm voice or even in writing. Loud is only about volume and says nothing about opinions. Ask whether the sentence is about a person pushing a view or about how much sound something makes.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    quiet: making little sound, easy to miss

    soft: low in volume, gentle on the ear

    Where it sits · how much sound reaches the ear

    noisy → loud → deafening

    noisy vs. loud: Noisy describes a place or a thing that keeps producing a mix of sounds — a noisy street, a noisy old car, a noisy class before the teacher comes in — and none of those sounds has to be strong on its own. Loud describes one sound that carries real volume: a loud bang, a loud voice. Ask whether the sentence is about many sounds going on at once or about the volume of one sound.

    deafening vs. loud: Deafening goes far beyond loud: the sound is so great that the ears cannot take it and, for a moment, nothing else can be heard — a jet taking off right beside you, an explosion. Loud means the sound carries and is hard to ignore, but people can still talk over it if they raise their voices. Ask whether the sentence says people simply heard the sound clearly or says their ears hurt and nothing else could be heard.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Loud is a plain, everyday word, and it measures one thing: volume. It fits a single sound (a loud crash), a voice (a loud laugh), a person who talks at high volume, or a place filled with sound (a loud party). It says nothing about whether the sound is pleasant or rough, so a loud song and a loud car crash are both loud. Two things to watch: turning the volume up is not the same as speaking so that people can understand you, and a person who keeps pushing an opinion is vocal, this unit's own word, whether or not any volume is involved. Loud also has a second meaning for colors and clothes — a loud shirt is one so bright and busy that it seems to scream for attention — but that is not the sense taught here.
