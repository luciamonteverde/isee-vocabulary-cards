multilingual

mul-tee-LING-gwul

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Multilingual describes a person who can speak and understand more than two languages, or a place, sign, or document that uses more than two languages together. The word is about counting: not one language spoken beautifully, not a pair, but several — three, four, or more — actually in use. A multilingual receptionist can greet a guest in whichever of her languages the guest speaks; a multilingual sign carries the same warning three or four times, once per language.

See the word in context

The airport's multilingual signs repeat every direction in four languages, from Mandarin to Arabic, so most travelers can find the trains without asking.

Because her grandmother speaks only Tagalog, her father grew up in Mexico, and her friends text in English, Maya is multilingual without ever having taken a language class.

Maya uses three languages — Tagalog with her grandmother, Spanish with her father's family, English with her friends — and she can switch among them as the person in front of her changes. That count, more than two languages actually in use, is what makes her multilingual. If she spoke only Spanish and English, the word would be bilingual; if she spoke English very well and nothing else, the word for that skill would be fluent, and multilingual would be wrong.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    fluent: Fluent describes how smoothly and easily a person handles one language — no groping for words, no long pauses — and it says nothing about how many languages that person has. You can be fluent in a single language, even your own. Multilingual is about the count and says nothing about smoothness. Ask whether the sentence is about how well one language is spoken, or how many languages are in use.

    multicultural: Multicultural describes a place or group where people of many backgrounds live together, with their different foods, holidays, music, and customs — and they may all speak the same language. Multilingual is only about languages: several are actually spoken or written there. Ask whether the sentence is about many ways of life side by side, or many languages in use.

    diverse: Diverse means made up of many different kinds — a diverse menu, a diverse team, a diverse set of opinions — and the kinds can be anything at all. Multilingual is narrow: the many different things are languages, and they are in use. Ask whether the sentence is about variety of any kind, or specifically about more than two languages being spoken or written.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    monolingual: speaking or using only one language

    English-only: offered or allowed in a single language, with no other version

    Where it sits · how much of the wider world the word takes in

    bilingual → multilingual → cosmopolitan

    bilingual vs. multilingual: Bilingual means exactly two languages — the 'bi-' is the same as in bicycle — and it is the precise word for a person or a sign with Spanish and English, or French and Arabic, and nothing more. Multilingual starts at three. Ask how many languages the sentence names or implies: two, or more than two.

    cosmopolitan vs. multilingual: Cosmopolitan describes a person or a city at home in many countries' ways of life — the food, the manners, the ideas, the people — and comfortable anywhere in the world. That is a far bigger claim than knowing several languages; a cosmopolitan traveler might get by in one. Multilingual claims only the languages. Ask whether the sentence is about comfort with many countries' ways of life, or about the number of languages someone uses.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Multilingual is a plain, neutral, slightly technical word: it turns up in job postings ('multilingual staff needed'), on packaging ('multilingual instructions inside'), and in school reports. It says how many languages, not how well — a multilingual guide might speak four languages, two of them roughly. Say bilingual when there are exactly two. And notice that the word works for things as well as people: a multilingual city, a multilingual website, a multilingual classroom are all places where more than two languages are in use at once.
