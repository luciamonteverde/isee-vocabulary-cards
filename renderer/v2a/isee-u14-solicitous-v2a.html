solicitous

suh-LIS-ih-tus

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Solicitous describes someone who shows worried, active care for another person's comfort or well-being — asking how they feel, fetching what they might need, checking on them again and again — so that the care is visible in what they do and carries a small note of anxiety.

See the word in context

The flight attendant was so solicitous toward the nervous first-time flyer that she stopped by his row with water, a blanket, and a calming word every twenty minutes.

All through the dinner party our host was solicitous toward her ninety-year-old uncle — moving his chair out of the draft, asking twice whether the soup was too hot, and hurrying back with a cushion he had not asked for.

The host is not merely well-mannered; she is worried about one particular person's comfort and keeps acting on that worry — the chair, the soup, the cushion. Care that shows up in repeated, anxious attention to someone's well-being is what solicitous means. A host who simply said 'Make yourself at home' would not have earned the word.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    attentive: Attentive means paying close attention — an attentive student follows every word of the lesson, an attentive waiter notices the empty glass. It is about noticing, and it needs no worry for anyone's welfare. Solicitous adds the worry: the attention is aimed at a person's comfort and carries some anxiety about it. Ask whether the person is noticing things or fretting over somebody's well-being.

    protective: Protective means guarding someone from harm or danger — a protective older brother walks his sister to the bus stop, a protective coach pulls an injured player out of the game. The care faces outward, toward a threat. Solicitous faces inward, toward comfort: is the guest warm enough, has the patient eaten. Ask whether the person is keeping harm away or looking after comfort.

    doting: Doting means extravagantly, uncritically fond — doting grandparents are sure the baby can do no wrong and cannot stop admiring it. Doting is about pride and adoration, and a person can dote from across the room. Solicitous is about worried, active care, and it needs no fondness at all — a solicitous waiter fusses over a total stranger. Ask whether the sentence shows adoration or shows anxious attention to someone's comfort.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    neglectful: failing to look after someone who needs care

    indifferent: not caring one way or the other how someone is doing

    Where it sits · how much active, worried attention someone's comfort gets

    polite → considerate → solicitous → smothering

    polite vs. solicitous: Polite is the lowest rung — good manners, 'please' and 'thank you,' the door held open — and it asks nothing about how the other person actually feels. Solicitous goes far beyond manners into worried, repeated care for one person's comfort. Ask whether the person is following the rules of courtesy or genuinely fussing over someone.

    considerate vs. solicitous: Considerate is the middle rung — a considerate person thinks about how their actions affect others and adjusts: turning down the music, saving a seat, not asking a hard question in public. It is quiet, it is a habit, and it is aimed at everyone. Solicitous is louder and narrower: worried attention fixed on one particular person, shown again and again. (On the test, considerate is the closest single match for solicitous.) Ask whether the care is a quiet habit toward people in general or anxious attention on someone in particular.

    smothering vs. solicitous: Smothering is the top rung — so much attention that the person on the receiving end cannot breathe: hovering, interrupting, refusing to let them do anything alone. Solicitous care is welcome, or at least easy to put up with; smothering care is resented. Ask whether the person being cared for wants it to stop.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    formal

    Solicitous is a formal word, most often used in the pattern 'solicitous toward someone' or 'solicitous about someone's health,' and it usually praises: a solicitous nurse, a solicitous host. Said with a raised eyebrow, it can hint that the care is a little much. Do not confuse it with the verb solicit, 'to ask for' — a solicitous person offers, and asks only how you are. On the test, expect considerate as its closest match; the difference this card teaches is that considerate is quiet and general, while solicitous is worried attention fixed on one person. Nearby in this unit, indulgent describes giving or allowing someone too much; solicitous gives attention, not treats.
