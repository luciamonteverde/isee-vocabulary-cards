perish

PAIR-ish

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

To perish is to lose your life to a disaster, to violence or to harsh conditions — a fire, a shipwreck, a winter with no food. It is the serious, formal word a history book or a news report uses, and it paints a hard end, not a peaceful one at home. Often many lives are lost at once: a crew, a village, an army.

See the word in context

Nearly half the settlers perished during that first winter, when the food ran out long before the snow did.

Thirty-one miners perished when the tunnel roof collapsed, and the town never reopened the mine.

This is perish because of how the lives were lost and how the sentence tells it: a sudden disaster underground took thirty-one people at once, and the writing is the serious voice of a news report or a history book. Nothing is softened, so pass away would be wrong; nobody fought a long illness, so succumb would be wrong. Died would be true, but perish tells you the deaths came from disaster.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    succumb: To succumb is to lose a fight — against an illness, a wound, the cold — and stop resisting, and it is always succumb TO something: there was a struggle first, sometimes a long one. When the fight is with illness or injury, to succumb is to die slowly, after resisting. Perish needs no struggle at all: the sailors perish the moment the ship goes down. Ask whether the sentence shows a fight that was slowly lost, or a life taken all at once by a disaster or hard conditions.

    expire: To expire is the quiet, official word for dying — a doctor's or a form's word, 'the patient expired at 3 a.m.' — and the same word is used when time runs out on a passport or a carton of milk. It pictures a life ending in a bed, on the clock. Perish is for deaths that disaster or harsh conditions cause. Ask whether the end was quiet and official, or brought on by fire, water, cold or violence.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    survive: to stay alive through danger

    endure: to last through hardship without giving way

    Where it sits · how harsh the death sounds

    pass away → die → perish

    pass away vs. perish: Pass away is the gentle way to say someone died, the words a family uses about a grandmother who died in old age; it wraps the death in kindness and almost always describes one person, at home or in a hospital. Perish does the opposite: it is for lives lost to disaster, war or wilderness, often many at once, and it wraps nothing in kindness. Ask whether the sentence is being gentle about one person's natural death, or reporting lives lost to disaster.

    die vs. perish: Die is the plain word for any death, from any cause, in any kind of writing, and it always fits where perish does. Perish is the narrower, more formal choice, kept for lives lost to disaster, violence or harsh conditions. Ask whether the sentence is plain, everyday talk about a death — then die — or a serious account of lives lost to a fire, a flood, a famine or a war.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Perish is formal and a little old-fashioned. You meet it in history books, on memorials, and in news reports of disasters — 'twelve climbers perished on the mountain' — and it is never the word for a pet hamster or a great-aunt who died quietly at ninety-four. Two smaller uses are worth knowing: things can perish — old rubber and old empires 'perish' when they rot or fall apart — and 'perish the thought' means 'may that never happen'. This card teaches the death sense, the one the exam tests. Its unit-mates devastating and ruin describe the blow and the loss it leaves; perish is what happens to the people caught in it.
