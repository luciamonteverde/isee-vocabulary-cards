leach

LEECH

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

To leach is to slowly wash a substance out of soil, rock, or another material by having water or another liquid pass through it: the liquid picks up salts, minerals, or chemicals as it moves and carries them away, a little at a time, so that what is left behind holds less and less of them.

See the word in context

Years of heavy rain had leached the nutrients out of the field, so the farmer had to spread fertilizer before planting.

Every storm leached chemicals from the old landfill into the creek below, and by summer the water had turned a strange orange.

The rain does not scoop the chemicals up or pour them out all at once. It works down through the buried trash, picks up what it can carry, and moves on into the creek — storm after storm, a little more each time. That slow carrying-away by a liquid passing through is what makes this leaching rather than spilling or dumping.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    drain: To drain is to let the liquid itself run out of something — drain a bathtub, drain a swamp, drain the pasta. Drain is the everyday cousin and the closest ordinary word, but it removes the water; leach removes what the water carries. Ask whether the sentence is about the liquid leaving or about a substance the liquid takes away with it.

    dissolve: To dissolve is for a solid to mix into a liquid until it disappears, like sugar in tea — and it stays right there in the liquid. Leaching starts with dissolving but does not stop there: the liquid then moves on through the material and carries the dissolved stuff out. Ask whether anything travels out of the material or whether it just melts into the liquid where it sits.

    rinse: To rinse is to run clean water over the surface of something quickly to wash off what is on it — rinse a plate, rinse your hands. Leaching is slow, works from inside the material, and usually happens on its own rather than on purpose. Ask whether the washing is a quick surface job someone chose to do or a slow soak-through that takes what is inside.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    absorb: to take a liquid or substance in and hold on to it

    deposit: to leave a substance behind as it settles

    Where it sits · how much the liquid carries away, and how fast

    soak → leach → flush

    soak vs. leach: When you soak something, liquid moves in and stays there — a sponge full of water, a lawn after rain — and nothing is taken out. Leaching is the next step: the liquid keeps moving through and takes dissolved salts or minerals out with it. Ask whether the liquid is only going in or whether it is passing through and carrying something away.

    flush vs. leach: To flush is to send a sudden rush of liquid through something so that whatever is inside is swept out all at once — flush a pipe, flush a toilet. Leaching does the same kind of job slowly and gently, a little with each pass of liquid. Ask whether the removal is one quick rush or a gradual carrying-away over time.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Leach is a science word: you will meet it in earth science, gardening, and environmental news — minerals leach from rock, nutrients leach from soil, chemicals leach into groundwater. It always needs a liquid doing the work and something dissolved being carried out. Do not confuse it with leech, the small worm that sticks to your skin and feeds on blood; the two sound exactly the same, but a leech takes blood while rain leaches salts. Leach is usually neutral or a little negative, since what gets carried away is often something you wanted to keep.
