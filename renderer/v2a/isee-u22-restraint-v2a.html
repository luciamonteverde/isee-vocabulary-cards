restraint

rih-STRAYNT

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Restraint is holding yourself back — keeping a reaction, an urge, or a display smaller and quieter than it could have been, or not acting on it at all: the biting reply you do not make, the second dessert you do not take. It is about the holding back itself, in the moment or in a whole style; what is being held in check is your own impulse, not someone else's.

See the word in context

When the referee missed the clear foul, the point guard kept her restraint, folding her arms instead of shouting.

With her first paycheck in hand at the mall, Dana exercised restraint: she bought the one pair of shoes she had planned on and walked past everything else.

Dana's restraint is the holding back itself: the urge to spend was there — a paycheck, a mall full of things — and she kept it in check on this occasion, buying less than she could have. Notice that no one stopped her and nothing was forbidden; the check came from inside. Your own urge, held back, this time — that is restraint.

    Accuracy

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    self-control: Self-control is the ability inside a person to govern their own impulses — a trait you have or lack, on show in every situation. Restraint is the holding back itself, seen on one occasion or in one piece of work: a reply you do not make, a room decorated in three colors instead of ten. It can even belong to a film or a design, which has no self to control. Ask whether the sentence describes a person's general ability to govern themselves or a particular act of holding back; only the second is restraint.

    moderation: Moderation is keeping to a sensible middle amount — not too much, not too little — as a habit over time: moderation in eating, spending, exercise. Restraint is about a specific urge that gets held back or cut down, often a reaction under pressure. Ask whether the sentence is about a balanced habit of 'not too much' or about someone holding back a particular impulse; only the second is restraint.

    composure: Composure is staying calm and steady on the outside when something is testing you — voice level, face steady. Restraint is about not acting on an urge, and you can have one without the other: a coach can be red-faced and shaking, composure gone, and still show restraint by not stepping onto the court. Ask whether the sentence is about staying calm or about not doing something one wanted to do; only the second is restraint.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    indulgence: giving in to an urge and enjoying it

    excess: more than is reasonable, with nothing held back

    Where it sits · how firmly and how long an urge is held back

    hesitation → restraint → abstinence

    hesitation vs. restraint: Hesitation is a pause before acting — the hand stops over the cookie, the finger over the send button — and it often ends with the act going ahead anyway. Restraint means the urge is held back for good, this time: the cookie stays on the plate. Ask whether the person paused and then acted or held back and did not; only the second is restraint.

    abstinence vs. restraint: Abstinence goes further than restraint: a person gives up a pleasure completely and keeps it up as a settled practice — no sweets at all, for a whole year. Restraint is the single act of holding back, and it need not be total or lasting; you can show restraint at one buffet and eat freely at the next. Ask whether the sentence describes one occasion of holding back or a firm, ongoing practice of going without; only the first is restraint.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Restraint is a word of praise in most sentences — it names the strength to hold back when acting would be easy: to show restraint, to exercise restraint, to answer with restraint. It also names holding back as a style: a designer's restraint, a writer's restraint, meaning less was used than could have been. Keep two other uses in mind. Restraint can be a thing that physically holds a person or object in place — a seat belt is a restraint, and a struggling patient may be put in restraints — which is the sense the verb restrain usually carries and the one your unit-mate stifle compares itself to. And a restraint on spending or on trade is a limit set by rules. This card teaches self-restraint, the sense your unit is about.
