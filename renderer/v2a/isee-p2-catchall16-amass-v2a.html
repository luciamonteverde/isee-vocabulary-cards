amass

uh-MASS -- from French/Latin a- ('to') + massa ('a lump, mass').

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

To amass is to deliberately build up a large amount of something, gathering it gradually over time.

See the word in context

The startup's founders spent five years quietly amassing enough patents to make the company worth acquiring.

By reinvesting every dollar of profit for a decade instead of spending it, the shop owner slowly amassed enough savings to open two more locations.

That slow, steady buildup over a long stretch of time -- not a single lucky windfall -- is exactly what it means to amass something.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    accumulate: To accumulate is for things to pile up bit by bit, often on their own -- dust accumulates on a shelf, interest accumulates in an account. To amass takes a person deliberately building the pile, and the pile is large: you amass a fortune; dust never does.

    stockpile: To stockpile is to store up supplies on purpose for a specific future need -- canned food before a long winter, batteries before a blackout. To amass points at the sheer size of what slowly builds up; to stockpile points at the plan behind the storing.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    disperse: to scatter or spread something out

    squander: to waste something carelessly

    Where it sits · how large a store builds up

    gather → amass → hoard

    gather vs. amass: Gathering is milder than amassing: you gather firewood for one evening's campfire, while amassing means piling up a truly large store steadily over months or years.

    hoard vs. amass: Hoarding goes further than amassing: a person who amasses builds up a large store over time, but a person who hoards keeps piling things up and will not let a single one go, long past any use or purpose.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Amass almost always implies a gradual buildup over time, whether of money, objects, or even less tangible things like power or evidence, rather than a single sudden acquisition.
