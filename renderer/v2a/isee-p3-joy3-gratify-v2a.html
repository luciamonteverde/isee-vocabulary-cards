gratify

GRAT-uh-fy

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

To give a person exactly what they were already wishing for — a hope, a craving, a curiosity, a hunger for praise — so that the wish is met and the person comes away glad. Gratify needs a wish that was there first: a glad surprise out of nowhere is not gratifying, and a need that is merely covered, with no gladness in it, is not gratified either.

See the word in context

Nothing gratified the old coach more than a call from a former player saying that all those early-morning drills had mattered after all.

On the last day of the unit, the science teacher finally gratified our curiosity and opened the sealed box that had sat on her desk since September.

The class had a wish that was there first — weeks of wondering what was in the box — and the teacher gave exactly that: the answer. The wish is met and the students come away glad, which is the whole shape of gratify. Had she brought in cupcakes nobody had asked for, that would please the class without gratifying anything; had she simply carried the box to the front office because the rules required it, that would satisfy a requirement with no gladness in it.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    please: the everyday word for making someone glad, and it needs no wish beforehand — a compliment out of the blue, a sunny morning, a kind stranger can all please. Gratify gives a person something they were already wanting. Ask whether the sentence shows a wish that was there first — a hope, a craving, a question they were dying to have answered; if the gladness arrives out of nowhere, the word is please.

    satisfy: to meet a need or a requirement, and it can be cold — a form satisfies the rules, a sandwich satisfies hunger, with nobody smiling. Gratify always leaves a person glad. Ask whether the sentence shows someone's gladness or only a need being covered; a box ticked with no one made happy is satisfy.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    deny: to refuse someone the thing they wanted

    disappoint: the hoped-for thing never arrives

    Where it sits · how far someone goes in giving another person what they want

    humor → gratify → indulge → spoil

    humor vs. gratify: to humor someone is to go along with a wish without truly giving in to it — nodding through a story you have heard ten times, agreeing to 'think about' the puppy. Gratify actually hands over the thing that was wanted. Ask whether the person ends up with what they asked for; if they got only a nod and a smile, the word is humor.

    indulge vs. gratify: to indulge is to give in to a wish that probably should have been refused — a second dessert, a third night off homework — and it comes with a raised eyebrow. Gratify carries no disapproval. Ask whether the sentence hints that giving in was unwise or too much; if it does, the word is indulge.

    spoil vs. gratify: to spoil is to give in so often that the person is harmed by it — a child who has never once heard the word no. Gratify is one wish met, with no damage done. Ask whether the sentence is about a pattern that has done harm; if so, it is spoil.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Gratify is a slightly formal word, and it turns up most often as gratified — 'she was gratified to see the room full' — or as gratifying. You can gratify a person or the wish itself: gratify a child, gratify a whim, gratify your curiosity. What gets gratified is always something that was wanted before it arrived. Do not confuse it with gratitude, which is thankfulness; the two share the Latin gratus, 'pleasing,' but a grateful person gives thanks, while a gratified person has received what they hoped for.
