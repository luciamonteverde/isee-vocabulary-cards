visualize

VIZH-oo-uh-lyze

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

To visualize something is to build a clear image of it in your mind on purpose — to see it in your head, in detail, as if it were right in front of you — whether it is a place across town, a routine you are about to perform, or a house that has not been built yet.

See the word in context

Before every dive, Keisha shuts her eyes on the platform and visualizes the whole thing — the jump, the two turns, the quiet slice into the water.

The carpenter could visualize the finished bookshelf so clearly that he cut every piece of wood without once drawing a plan.

There is no bookshelf yet — only a stack of cut wood. The carpenter builds the picture himself, on purpose, and in enough detail to measure from: the height, the number of shelves, where each piece meets the next. Nothing outside him put that picture there; he made it, and he held it steady while he worked. That is visualize — the person deliberately forms the image and sees it as if it stood in front of him.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    envision: To envision something is to picture how it could turn out in the future — a career, a finished city, a better school — and it stays big-picture: you envision the shape of a possibility, not every brick. Visualize builds the actual image in detail, and it can be of something right here and now, like the route to the store. Ask whether the sentence is about seeing a possible future in broad outline, or about forming a detailed picture in the mind.

    foresee: To foresee something is to know ahead of time that it is going to happen — a claim about what will come true, and no picture is required: a coach can foresee a loss without seeing it in her head. Visualize makes no claim about what will happen; a pitcher can visualize a perfect throw and then miss. Ask whether the sentence says the person knew what was coming, or that the person built a picture in the mind.

    evoke: To evoke is for an outside thing — a smell, a song, a painting — to bring a picture or a feeling to life in someone's mind without being asked. The person is on the receiving end. Visualize is the person's own act: they sit down and build the picture on purpose. Ask who did the work — something outside the person, or the person's own effort.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    overlook: fail to see something even when it is right in front of you

    forget: lose the picture of something from the mind

    Where it sits · how sharply and steadily the mind holds the image

    imagine → picture → visualize

    imagine vs. visualize: Imagine is the broadest word — the mind forms an idea of something that is not there, and the idea need not be a picture at all: you can imagine a sound, a story, a rule, or how someone feels. Visualize means the idea takes the form of an image you see in your head. Ask whether the sentence requires a picture seen in the mind, or any idea of something not present.

    picture vs. visualize: Picture is the middle word — an image comes to mind, often in a flash: 'I can just picture Dad's face when he opens it.' It is a glimpse, and it comes easily. Visualize is deliberate work: the person builds the image in detail and holds it long enough to use — to measure from it, to rehearse it. Ask whether the image simply flashed into the mind, or was built and held on purpose.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Visualize is a deliberate, working word. Athletes visualize a routine before they perform it, architects visualize a building before it exists, a chess player visualizes the board three moves ahead — in every case the person is doing the picturing, on purpose, as a tool. It is spelled -ize in US English (British writing uses -ise). Its relatives are visual, vision and visible. Do not confuse it with evoke, taught in this same unit: when a song evokes a summer, the song does the work and the feeling arrives on its own; when you visualize a summer, you sit down and build the picture yourself. On the ISEE expect visualize in sentence completion, in a sentence about planning or imagining something not yet there.
