What It Means

To stymie is to block the progress of something with an obstacle solid enough to hold it up until a way around is found.

See the word in context

Budget cuts at the state level stymied the school district's plan to hire three new music teachers.

The archaeological dig was stymied for months after ground-penetrating radar revealed an unmapped water pipe running directly beneath the most promising excavation site.

The dig wasn't cancelled outright -- it was simply slowed and blocked by an unexpected obstacle, which is the everyday sense of stymie: hindering progress rather than ending it completely.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    hinder: To hinder is the plain, neutral word for making progress harder or slower, and a hindrance can be small -- one missing form can hinder an application. To stymie is stronger and more conversational: a stymied project has hit an obstacle solid enough to hold it up for a long stretch.

    handicap: To handicap is to load someone or something with a lasting disadvantage that must be carried the whole way -- outdated equipment handicaps a factory year after year. To stymie points instead to a specific obstacle in the road: something has blocked the plan's progress, often suddenly, until a way around is found.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    facilitate: to make an action or process easier

    expedite: to make a process happen faster

    Where it sits · how badly plans get held up

    slow → stymie → derail

    slow vs. stymie: To slow a project is only a mild change of pace -- much milder than to stymie it, which plants a real obstacle in its path and holds its progress up.

    derail vs. stymie: To derail a plan is to knock it completely off track, a more total failure than being merely stymied.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Stymie is a fairly informal, conversational word for being blocked -- you'll hear it in everyday speech about plans, projects, or negotiations much more than in formal writing. Compare it with thwart, which usually describes stopping one specific attempt outright rather than slowly hindering an ongoing effort.
