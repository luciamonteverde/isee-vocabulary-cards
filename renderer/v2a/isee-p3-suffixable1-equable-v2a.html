What It Means

Equable is calm and steady in temperament, showing no extreme highs or lows whether things go well or badly.

See the word in context

Growing up on a working farm, where some crisis always needed fixing before breakfast, taught Marisol to develop an equable outlook that later made her an excellent emergency-room nurse.

No matter how many last-minute schedule changes the tour company threw at him, the bus driver's equable response was always the same patient shrug and a new plan.

Equable describes a steady temperament that isn't rattled by stress or setbacks -- not the absence of feeling, just a lack of extreme highs and lows.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    even-tempered: Even-tempered means not easily upset, keeping a level mood from day to day -- it is the plain, everyday way of saying what equable says. Equable is the more formal word and stretches further: an equable climate, like an equable person, avoids extremes in both directions.

    unflappable: Unflappable praises composure when things go wrong -- an unflappable guide cannot be rattled by an emergency. It says nothing about staying level when things go right. Equable covers both directions: an equable temperament has no sharp highs or lows, in good news or bad.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    volatile: liable to sudden, unpredictable changes in mood

    excitable: easily agitated or aroused to strong feeling

    Where it sits · how firmly composure holds under pressure

    mild-mannered → equable → unshakeable

    mild-mannered vs. equable: Mild-mannered is gentler than equable: it describes a generally calm style, while an equable person goes further, staying steady and unruffled even under real pressure.

    unshakeable vs. equable: Unshakeable composure goes even further than equable -- nothing at all can disturb it, even in a genuine crisis.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Equable is a quiet compliment for someone's temperament -- it praises steadiness, not excitement. English also uses this same word for climate ('an equable climate' -- one without extreme heat or cold), so don't be surprised to see it applied to weather as well as people.
