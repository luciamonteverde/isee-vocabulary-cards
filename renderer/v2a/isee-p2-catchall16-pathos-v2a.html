pathos

PAY-thahs -- from Greek pathos ('suffering, emotion, feeling').

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Pathos is a quality in a speech, story, or artwork that makes a deep, insistent appeal to the audience's real pity or grief.

See the word in context

The mayor's speech leaned on pathos, describing the flood survivors huddled together on their rooftops as the water rose.

Instead of listing statistics about hunger, the charity's ad used pure pathos, showing a single child's empty lunchbox in complete silence.

That deliberate appeal to the audience's real pity or grief, rather than facts or logic, is exactly what pathos describes.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    anguish: Anguish is raw, overwhelming suffering felt by a person -- the emotion itself. Pathos is not the pain but the quality in a speech, story, or image that makes an audience pity that pain. A documentary can show anguish; its pathos is what makes viewers ache.

    sentiment: Sentiment is tender, comfortable feeling or its expression -- a greeting card, a warm wedding toast. Pathos is sharper and heavier: the quality in a work that stirs an audience's real pity or grief, not just a pleasant glow.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    levity: light, humorous treatment of a subject

    detachment: emotional distance and a lack of feeling

    Where it sits · how strongly it tugs at pity

    poignancy → pathos → melodrama

    poignancy vs. pathos: Poignancy is gentler than pathos: an old man feeding pigeons alone has a quiet poignancy, while pathos is a deeper, more insistent pull on the audience's pity.

    melodrama vs. pathos: Melodrama is pathos pushed past believing: pathos tugs sincerely at an audience's pity, while melodrama piles on so much exaggerated emotion that viewers stop feeling and start giggling.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Pathos is one of the three classical rhetorical appeals, alongside ethos and logos (both taught earlier in this course) -- they are not synonyms of one another. Also contrast pathos with cathartic, taught earlier in this unit: cathartic describes your own sense of emotional release, while pathos describes a quality a speech, story, or artwork uses to move its audience.
