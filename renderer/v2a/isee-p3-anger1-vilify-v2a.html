Meaning

What It Means

To vilify is to speak or write ill of someone publicly and unfairly, attacking their whole character and reputation.

See the word in context

The scientist was vilified in the press for years before later research proved his theory correct.

After the team's shocking loss, sports commentators spent the entire week vilifying the rookie quarterback, blaming him personally for every missed play.

Vilify means to speak or write ill of someone publicly and unfairly -- the commentators are actively damaging the quarterback's reputation.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    defame: To defame is to damage someone's reputation by making false statements about them -- falsity is the heart of it, and defamation is the word courts use. To vilify is broader: it counts even when every fact used against them is technically true, so long as the attack is public and unfair.

    slander: To slander is to damage a reputation with false SPOKEN statements -- put the same falsehood in print and the legal word changes. Vilify carries no such requirements: you can vilify someone in speech or writing, and the attack need not be false at all, only hostile and unfair.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    praise: to express approval or admiration

    laud: to praise highly

    Where it sits · how badly the reputation gets attacked

    criticize → vilify → demonize

    criticize vs. vilify: To criticize is far milder than to vilify -- criticism points out particular faults, while vilifying someone means attacking their whole character and reputation in public.

    demonize vs. vilify: To demonize someone is to portray them as evil -- an even more extreme, distorted form of vilifying them.

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Vilify is the act of attacking someone's reputation through words, publicly and unfairly -- distinct from upbraid (a direct scolding for a specific fault) and umbrage (the feeling of being offended), and milder in scope than the underlying feeling of rancor.
