virtuoso

vur-choo-OH-so -- from Italian virtuoso, 'skilled,' from Latin virtus, 'excellence, skill': a virtuoso has excellence others can only admire.

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

A virtuoso is a person of dazzling, extraordinary skill, especially in a musical or artistic field.

See the word in context

Critics agreed that the fifteen-year-old cellist played like a true virtuoso, drawing a standing ovation from an audience twice her age.

Without any formal training, the shipyard welder became a virtuoso of his craft, welding seams so flawless that engineers flew in from across the country just to study his technique.

A virtuoso doesn't have to work in music or art -- the word describes anyone whose skill in a craft has become extraordinary, even a welder whose seams other experts travel across the country to study.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    expert: An expert has deep knowledge and reliable skill in a field -- the person you trust to do the job right. Virtuoso raises the bar from competence to performance: a virtuoso's skill is so dazzling that watching it feels like a show. Every virtuoso is an expert; few experts are virtuosos.

    maestro: Maestro is a respectful title for a master of music, especially a great conductor, and it rarely leaves the concert hall. Virtuoso also began in music but now travels anywhere: a chef or a glassblower can be a virtuoso, while maestro would sound odd outside the arts.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    amateur: someone who does something for enjoyment, without professional skill

    novice: a beginner with little experience

    Where it sits · how dazzling the skill on display is

    master → virtuoso → prodigy

    master vs. virtuoso: Master describes complete command of a skill earned through long practice, while virtuoso adds a sense of dazzling, almost theatrical brilliance on top of that mastery.

    prodigy vs. virtuoso: Prodigy goes even further than virtuoso by suggesting the talent is natural and appeared unusually early, often in childhood, rather than built entirely through years of practice.
