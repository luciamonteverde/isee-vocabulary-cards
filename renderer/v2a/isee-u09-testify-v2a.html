testify

TES-tuh-fy

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

To testify is to give evidence as a person speaking in a formal proceeding — a courtroom, a hearing, a committee — under oath, so that what you say becomes part of the official record and lying in it is itself a crime. You testify about what you saw, did, or know; the oath and the setting are what make it testimony rather than ordinary telling.

See the word in context

The mechanic was called to testify at the hearing and answered every question about the brakes after taking the oath.

Though her hands shook, the bakery owner agreed to testify at the trial, telling the jury under oath exactly when the delivery truck struck her sign.

She is testifying because she is a person speaking in a formal proceeding — a trial, before a jury — after taking an oath, so her words go on the record and a lie would be a crime. Telling the same story to a friend over coffee would not be testifying, and a photograph of the broken sign cannot testify at all. A sworn account, given in the proceeding, by a person: that is testify.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    vouch: Vouch means to guarantee a person or a claim on your own say-so — you 'vouch for' a friend at the door — putting your own reputation and your own experience on the line, with no oath and no court. Testify puts the account on an official record, under oath, where a lie is a crime. Ask whether the backing is a personal promise given in everyday life, or sworn evidence in a proceeding.

    witness: To witness is to see something happen with your own eyes, at the time it happens. To testify is what that person may do afterward: give a sworn account of it in a proceeding. Ask whether the sentence is about seeing the event or about telling it under oath later.

    swear: Swear is the oath itself — you swear to tell the truth — or a strong promise in everyday talk: 'I swear I locked the door.' Testify is the evidence you give after the oath, question by question, in the proceeding. Ask whether the sentence is about making the promise or about giving the account.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    deny: declare that something is not true

    conceal: hide what you know instead of telling it

    Where it sits · how formally a person backs a claim

    affirm → attest → testify

    affirm vs. testify: Affirm is the lowest of the three — to state something as a fact and stand behind it on your own authority, anywhere: a coach can affirm that the runner is fit. No oath, no proceeding, no record. Ask whether anything more than the speaker's own word is at stake; if not, the word is affirm.

    attest vs. testify: Attest is the middle step: backing a claim as true — you 'attest to' it — often from direct knowledge and sometimes in writing, but with no oath required, and a stamped passport or a worn staircase can attest to something as well as a person can. Testify is the top: a person, under oath, in a formal proceeding, on the record. Ask whether the sentence has all three — a person, an oath, a proceeding; if any one is missing, the word is attest.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Testify is the courtroom verb: people who saw something testify, and what they say is testimony. It takes 'about' or 'that' — testify about the accident, testify that the light was red — and 'testify against' or 'for' someone. Writers sometimes borrow it for things, as in 'the scars testify to the fight,' leaning on the courtroom picture as a figure of speech; the plain sense, and the one the ISEE tests, is a person giving sworn evidence in a proceeding. Its unit-mate certify is different: an authority can certify that something meets a standard, while a person testifies to what they know.
