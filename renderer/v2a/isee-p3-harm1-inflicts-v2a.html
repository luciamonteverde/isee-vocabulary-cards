Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

To inflict something — pain, damage, a punishment, a defeat — is to make someone or something suffer it: the harm is dealt out by one side and lands on the other, and the one dealing it can be a person, a storm, a disease, or a new rule.

See the word in context

A single careless comment can inflict more lasting hurt on a friendship than a whole year of small disagreements.

The new fee on street vendors inflicts real hardship on families who earn their whole living from a single cart.

The city set the fee, but the fee is not what is inflicted — the hardship is. Families now go without, and that suffering is dealt out by the rule and lands on them; harm made to fall on a receiver is exactly what inflict means. Notice that the subject is a rule, not a person: anything that makes others suffer can inflict, and no one has to intend the harm.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    administer: To administer is to hand something out officially, following a set procedure — a nurse administers a vaccine, a teacher administers a test, a judge administers an oath — and the thing handed out may be helpful or neutral. Inflict is only ever for harm, and it needs no procedure: a slammed door can inflict a bruise. Ask whether the sentence shows an official carrying out a set step (administer) or harm landing on someone, however it got there (inflict).

    unleash: To unleash is to let a powerful force go all at once — unleash a flood, unleash the dogs, unleash a storm of criticism — and the word points at the moment of release, not at anyone being hurt. Inflict points at the harm arriving: a wound, a loss, a defeat that someone now suffers. Ask whether the object of the verb is a force being set loose (unleash) or the harm itself being made to land (inflict).

    perpetrate: To perpetrate is to carry out a wrongful act — a fraud, a hoax, a robbery — and the object is the deed, done by someone who chose to do it. Inflict's object is the harm the victim suffers, and no one has to be at fault: a drought inflicts losses. Ask whether the sentence names a crime or trick being committed (perpetrate) or damage, pain, or loss landing on a victim (inflict).

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    spare: to hold back from harming someone

    relieve: to take pain or trouble away

    Where it sits · how much real damage lands on the receiver

    imposes → inflicts → wreaks

    imposes vs. inflicts: Impose is the lower step — to set something unwelcome on people from a position of authority: a fee, a curfew, a deadline, a rule. It may cost them nothing but freedom, and only someone in charge can do it. Inflict makes the receiver actually suffer harm, and anyone or anything can do it. Ask whether the sentence shows a rule or requirement being set by authority (impose) or harm, pain, or loss actually landing (inflict).

    wreaks vs. inflicts: Wreak is the far step — large-scale destruction or chaos, almost always paired with a word like 'havoc' or 'destruction' and never planned by anyone: a hurricane wreaks havoc, a virus wreaks havoc on a network. Inflict can be small and exact — one fine, one bruise, one lost game — and it can be deliberate. Ask whether the sentence describes wide, chaotic damage with no single victim in view (wreak) or a specific harm landing on a specific receiver (inflict).

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Inflict is a harm word: what gets inflicted is always something nobody wants — a wound, a loss, a penalty, a defeat, a cost. You inflict it ON someone: 'the frost inflicted heavy losses on the vineyards.' The word says nothing about blame; a bully, a hurricane, and a fair referee can all inflict something. The form on this list is inflicts, as in 'the summer sun inflicts real damage on unprotected skin.'
