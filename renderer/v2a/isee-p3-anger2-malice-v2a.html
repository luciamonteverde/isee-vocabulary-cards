malice

MAL-iss

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Malice is the wish to do real harm to someone — to see them hurt, shamed, or ruined — where the harm itself is the goal, wanted ahead of time, and no quarrel or injury is needed to set it off.

See the word in context

The night before the big pitch, a coworker deleted every one of Farah's slides out of pure malice, hoping to watch her fail in front of the whole company.

The old man had done nothing to the teenagers, but out of malice they poured salt over his vegetable beds one night, wanting him to find every plant dead by morning.

Notice what is missing: there was no quarrel, no insult, nothing the old man had done to them. The teenagers simply wanted him to suffer a loss, and they planned it so that he would. That forward-looking wish for real harm — the ruined garden is the point, not a side effect of anything — is malice. If they had merely disliked him, or complained about him, the word would not fit.

    Accuracy

    “Does this word fit the meaning exactly, or does a neighbour fit better?”

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    animosity: Animosity is open hostility toward a particular person — hard looks across a room, sharp words, a feud everyone can see — and it is a feeling, not a plan; two people can carry animosity for years without either one ever meaning to do the other real harm. Malice looks ahead to the harm and wants it. Ask whether the sentence shows hostile feeling on display (animosity) or a wish for someone's actual injury (malice).

    hatred: Hatred is intense dislike, so strong the person can hardly stand the thing hated — and you can hate a food, a song, or a tyrant you will never meet without wanting to hurt anything. Malice is narrower and colder: it is not how much you loathe someone but that you want them hurt. Ask whether the sentence is about the strength of a feeling (hatred) or about a wish to do harm (malice).

    resentment: Resentment is bitterness at being treated unfairly — it looks back at a wrong done to you and keeps it warm. Malice looks forward, toward damage it wants to cause, and it needs no wrong to start from: a stranger can be its target. Ask whether the sentence points back at a wrong the person suffered (resentment) or ahead at harm the person wants to cause (malice).

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    goodwill: a friendly wish for others to do well

    kindness: real care for other people's well-being

    Where it sits · how much harm the ill feeling means to do

    spite → malice → cruelty

    spite vs. malice: Spite is the lower step — a small, petty urge to sting someone back for a slight: hiding the remote, a dig in a reply, one name left off the invitation. It wants the other person annoyed, not harmed, and it always answers something. Malice wants real damage and needs no slight at all. Ask whether the sentence describes a petty jab in return for something (spite) or a wish to see genuine harm done (malice).

    cruelty vs. malice: Cruelty is the far step — the harm is not just wished for but carried out, and the person causing it enjoys the suffering. Malice can live entirely in the mind: a person full of malice may never lift a finger. Ask whether the sentence shows suffering being caused and enjoyed (cruelty) or a wish for harm, acted on or not (malice).

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Malice is a serious, slightly formal word, and it is the one courts use: 'with malice' means the harm was meant, not accidental, and that difference can decide a case. In everyday speech it turns up mostly when someone denies it — 'there was no malice in it' means a hurt was caused without anyone wanting it. The adjective is malicious. On the ISEE synonym section the closest match offered is animosity; see the Compare panel for how the two differ.
