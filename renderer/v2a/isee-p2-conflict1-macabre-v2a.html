macabre

muh-KAH-bruh -- from Old French danse macabre, 'Dance of Death'; origin disputed.

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

Macabre is horrifying in a way that is focused on death and built to disturb, even with no gore shown at all.

See the word in context

The museum's new exhibit on Victorian mourning rituals struck several visitors as unexpectedly macabre.

The walking tour turned macabre at its next stop, a plague-era cemetery where the guide described, in careful detail, exactly how the bodies had been stacked centuries ago.

That kind of death-focused detail -- meant to unsettle rather than simply inform -- is exactly what macabre describes.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    gruesome: Gruesome describes raw, graphic horror -- blood, wounds, vivid gory detail -- and anything shocking to look at can be gruesome. Macabre is tied to death and the atmosphere around it: a macabre story can chill you with no gore shown at all.

    grim: Grim means bleak, stern, and joyless -- a grim expression, a grim winter forecast -- with nothing horrifying required. Macabre goes much further into the dark: it is gruesome, death-focused, and built to disturb rather than merely sadden.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    cheerful: noticeably happy and lighthearted

    wholesome: promoting health, decency, and a sense of well-being

    Where it sits · how broadly the deathly horror applies

    grisly → macabre → ghoulish

    grisly vs. macabre: Grisly usually describes a specific gruesome sight or detail, like a grisly crime scene. Macabre is broader and can describe a whole mood, story, or exhibit as horrifying, even without one single graphic image.

    ghoulish vs. macabre: Ghoulish is close in strength to macabre, but it more often criticizes a person's unhealthy fascination with death, while macabre more neutrally describes the disturbing quality of a thing itself.
