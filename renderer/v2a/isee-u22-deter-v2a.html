dih-TUR

Section 1

Meaning

“What does this word mean?”

Hear It

What It Means

To deter someone is to stop them from doing something before they do it, by making the results of the act look too costly or dangerous to risk — a fine, a camera, a guard dog. The would-be actor sees what would follow and decides against the act themselves; no one has to argue with them, and nothing physically stops them.

See the word in context

A sign warning of a five-hundred-dollar fine was enough to deter most hikers from dumping trash at the edge of the park.

After coins kept disappearing from the tip jar, the bakery put a camera above the counter, and the camera deterred the thieves: not one coin has gone missing since.

The camera deters because anyone thinking of taking a coin now sees what would follow — being caught on film — and decides not to try. Notice what did not happen: nobody spoke to anyone, and the coins are still sitting in an open jar that a hand could reach. The act stayed possible; the threat of consequences made people choose against it. That is deter.

    Compare the words

    Synonyms · words with a similar meaning

    dissuade: Dissuade means to talk someone out of an action — a person makes the case, with reasons, usually face to face, and the listener changes their mind. Deter needs no conversation at all: a posted fine or a barking dog does the work, and the person simply weighs the risk and steps back. Ask whether anyone spoke to the person and argued; if all there was is a sign, a price, or a threat of what would follow, the word is deter.

    scare off: To scare off is to frighten someone away on the spot — they were already there, and fear sent them running. Deter works earlier and more quietly: the person thinks about what would happen and never starts. Ask whether the person fled from something right in front of them or decided in advance not to try; only the second is deter.

    What it isn’t · antonyms and near-opposites

    encourage: to make someone more willing to act

    tempt: to make an act look appealing enough to try

    Where it sits · how surely the action is kept from happening

    discourage → deter → prevent

    discourage vs. deter: Discourage is the everyday, broader word — it makes someone less willing or less hopeful about doing something, and it can happen before or during the effort: a hard first chapter can discourage a reader halfway through. Deter is narrower: it works only before the act, and only through the threat of what would follow. Ask whether the person was put off by consequences before they started; if they simply lost heart, or were already at it, the word is discourage.

    prevent vs. deter: Prevent is stronger and wider — it makes the act impossible or stops it by any means, including physically: a locked gate prevents the goats from wandering whether they want to or not. Deter leaves the act possible; it only makes the person choose not to. Ask whether the person could still have done it and decided against it; if they could not do it at all, the word is prevent.

    Section 3

    Apply

    “Can I recognize and use it correctly?”

    Word Choice

    Deter is a cool, practical word from the world of rules and security: cameras deter thieves, stiff fines deter speeding, a strong defense deters an attack. What deters is usually a thing or a threat rather than a person's plea, and it always looks forward — you cannot deter something that has already happened. Its noun is deterrent, the fine or the guard dog itself. On the ISEE the accepted match for deter is discourage, the safe broad word; keep deter for the narrower case where fear of what would follow does the work.
