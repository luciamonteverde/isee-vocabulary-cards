Supporting Detail: Locate and Match

Section 1

What this question looks like

On the test

A question that asks what the passage says about one specific thing - a person, an event, a reason, a number. All four answer choices sound like things the passage could have said. Only one of them is actually written there.

Ways it gets asked

  • According to the passage, ...
  • The passage states that ...
  • According to the author, ...
  • The passage indicates that ...
  • Which of the following does the passage mention as ...?

The one thing to remember

The answer is already written in the passage, so your job is to find that sentence and pick the choice that says the same thing in different words.

Section 2

How to do it

Step 1. Find the topic of the question

Ask what the question is about before you read the choices. Pick out the name or thing it names. Hunt for that word in the passage. You are not looking for the answer yet - only for where the passage talks about it.

Step 2. Go back to the passage and find that word

Run your eye down the passage looking only for your topic word, or a form of it. Names and numbers are easiest to spot. If the word appears more than once, check each place. The first one is not always the right one.

Step 3. Read the whole sentence before you look at the choices

Read the full sentence around your word, and the next one if the thought carries on. Then say what it means in your own plain words. Do this before you read the choices, or the one that sounds best will pull your eye.

Step 4. Match your sentence to the choice that says the same thing

Now read the four choices. Look for the one that means what your sentence means. Expect it to use different words from the passage. A choice that copies the passage's exact words is not automatically right - the test writers know you will recognise them.

Step 5. Check that the other three fail for a reason the passage settles

Check the other three choices against the passage. Each should fail for a reason you can point to: the passage says the opposite, says it about someone or something else, or never says it. If you cannot find that reason, go back to Step 2.

Section 3

Worked example one

A finished map is expensive to produce and inexpensive to appropriate. Surveyors must traverse the territory, measure it, and dispute it, while a copyist need only trace the sheet, alter the coloring, and publish. Because a fact about the world belongs to nobody, the law offered the original publisher little relief.

Their response was quietly ingenious. Cartographers began to salt their maps with deliberate errors: a thoroughfare that did not exist, a bend the river never made, an elevation granted height it had not earned, a hamlet whose name no resident recognized. An independent surveyor would never reproduce such fictitious details, since the terrain itself would contradict him, so their appearance in a rival atlas amounted to a confession. A careful publisher maintained a private legend of every falsehood, so the evidence could be produced in court.

The scheme had two weaknesses. Courts in several countries held that a spurious street became a fact the moment it was published, or that a handful of duplicated details demonstrated little, so the traps seldom prevailed in the cases they were designed for. And a fiction published with sufficient authority has a way of becoming true: one imaginary crossroads acquired a general store named after it, and the store, being genuine, eventually earned it a legitimate place on maps that had copied nothing. The mapmakers had set out to protect the truth and had, in the end, contributed a small piece to it.

Try it

    Why that is the answer

    Step 3 settles it: read the whole second-paragraph sentence - a private legend of every falsehood, produced in court, has to be a hidden list.

    • Not A. Checking the ground is the surveyor's job in the passage, not the publisher's - right idea, wrong person.
    • Not C. No copying warning is anywhere in the passage - a choice the passage never says cannot be right.
    • Not D. The third paragraph says the traps 'seldom prevailed' in court - the opposite of usually winning.

    'Pick the choice with the passage's own words' fails: 'rival atlas' and 'court' come straight from the text, and both sit in wrong choices.

    Section 4

    Worked example two

    The summer I turned thirteen, my mother decided I had an excess of idle time. She arranged for me to spend afternoons with Adair, who restored antique clocks in a shop behind the courthouse. I imagined myself at his bench within a week, coaxing some ancient mechanism back to life; what he imagined for me was the broom.

    In June I was permitted three occupations: sweeping filings from the floor, winding the clocks in the window, and carrying cleaned components to the bench on a square of felt. The movements themselves, the trains of wheels and the minute escapements ticking beneath his loupe, remained forbidden to me. When I sighed at the tedium, which was frequently, he did not acknowledge it, so I concluded he was either deaf or indifferent.

    In July a carriage clock arrived whose balance would not run true, and for a week he permitted me to observe. He would adjust one screw by a fraction, set it running, listen with his head inclined, then dismantle it entirely again. He remained unhurried and without complaint, as though the eleventh attempt were as absorbing as the first. When it finally ran, he nodded, wiped his fingers, and observed that the felt required washing.

    I did not become a clockmaker, but I have never since hurried through a tiresome task without hearing that clock, or suspecting that the old man had registered each of my sighs.

    Try it

      Why that is the answer

      Step 3 settles it: read the third-paragraph sentence to the end - 'observed' means said, 'felt' is the cloth, so he said the cloth needed washing.

      • Not A. The passage never says he let the narrator try - the last paragraph begins 'I did not become a clockmaker'.
      • Not B. The passage says the opposite - the eleventh attempt was 'as absorbing as the first', made 'without complaint'.
      • Not D. He did take it apart again, but during the week of attempts, before it ran - right action, wrong moment.

      'Pick the ending the story seems to build toward' fails: choice A is what the boy hoped for, but the passage never says it happened.

      Section 5

      What goes wrong, and your checklist

      Where marks get lost

      • Answering from memory instead of going back. Your memory of the passage is not evidence - the sentence is.
      • Stopping at the first place your word appears. Check every place it appears, not just the first.
      • Reading half the sentence. Read to the end - the detail you need often sits in the last few words.
      • Trusting a choice because it uses the passage's exact words. The right choice usually says the idea another way.
      • Picking what should have happened, or a fact about the wrong person or moment. Take only what the sentence says.

      Do this every time

      1. Find the topic word in the question, then find it in the passage.
      2. Read the whole sentence - to the end - before you look at the choices.
      3. Say it in your own words, then match the choice that means the same thing.
      4. Expect the right choice to use different words from the passage.
      5. Make sure each wrong choice fails for a reason you can point to in the passage.
