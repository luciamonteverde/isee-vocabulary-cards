What It Means

Laconic describes a way of speaking or writing that uses very few words.

Show it in context

The laconic ranger answered our five questions with two words each.

Here's how to decide when laconic is the exact word.

Worked Example

The executive's weekly emails contained only essential information, never a wasted word, so that her staff could scan them in 30 seconds.

This is laconic speech—it's not just short, it's deliberately minimal and efficient, using very few words as a deliberate choice. 'Brief' wouldn't capture this deliberate economy; laconic does.

Your Turn

A student forgot to do the homework, so he turned in three blank pages.

Is laconic the right word here?

Incomplete

Correct — The student simply did not do the homework, so the empty pages are incomplete work rather than an intentionally short, sharp answer.

Laconic

Not quite — Laconic means saying a lot with very few words on purpose, but three blank pages say nothing at all, so this is not a brief, pointed style.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
brief

Brief just means short in duration or length; laconic is deliberately using very few words, often with wit or style.

terse

Terse means using few words but can sound rude or abrupt; laconic is few words with an economical or even elegant style.

concise

Concise means efficient and to-the-point; laconic is similarly minimal but suggests an almost stark economy of words.

Antonyms · opposites

verbose — Verbose means using many words; laconic is the opposite—using only necessary words.

Build the Word

Lakonikos Greek: 'of Laconia, of the Spartans'

The ancient Spartans of Laconia were renowned for their pithy, minimal speech; laconic captures this famous sparse style of communication.

The Spartan soldiers communicated with minimal words during their military campaigns.

Same root, new word — what does Spartan mean here?

of or relating to Sparta; austere and minimalist

Correct — Correct. Spartan and laconic both come from Laconia, the region around Sparta, whose people were known for stripped-down, few-word speech.

loud, showy, and full of boasting

Not quite — The root points back to Laconia and Sparta, whose people were famous for being plain and brief, the opposite of loud and showy.

The root connection →

Laconic comes from Lakonikos, meaning of Laconia, the area around Sparta. The Spartans were famous for blunt, few-word replies, which is exactly what laconic describes.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / literary

Laconic describes a distinctive speaking or writing style—deliberate brevity that often has a certain elegance. Use laconic when someone uses very few words as a deliberate choice, not just when they happen to be quiet or short.
