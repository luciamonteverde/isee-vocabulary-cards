Despondent

/dih-SPON-dent/ — stress on the middle syllable · adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Despondent describes someone who feels deeply discouraged and has lost hope, often after a disappointment.

Show it in context

After failing his driving test for the third time, Marcus grew despondent and stopped practicing.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when despondent is the exact word.

Worked Example

After being rejected from her top five colleges, a senior stopped checking her email, withdrew from her friend group, and told her parents that college didn't matter anymore. She had become despondent.

Being 'sad' or even 'disappointed' doesn't capture what happened here. Despondent is more precise because it means she didn't just feel bad—she lost hope altogether and gave up trying. The disappointment crushed her spirit, and that total loss of hope is exactly what despondent means.

Your Turn

A chess player loses a single tournament game and feels frustrated about his mistakes.

Would 'despondent' describe how he feels, or is 'disappointed' better?

disappointed

Correct — Losing a single game is a setback he still expects to bounce back from, so 'disappointed' fits far better than crushing despair.

despondent

Not quite — Despondent demands deep hopelessness and despair, but he has lost only one game and hasn't given up on chess or on himself.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
discouraged

Feeling less motivated by setbacks. Discouraged is milder; despondent means hope itself is gone.

dejected

Sad and dispirited, with low morale. Dejected and despondent are close, but despondent adds the specific loss of hope.

hopeless

Seeing no way out. Hopeless is the endpoint of despondency; despondent is the emotional state that leads there.

Antonyms · opposites

optimistic — Hopeful and confident about the future — the opposite of the hopelessness in despondent.

How Strong Is It?
disappointed→discouraged→despondent→hopeless

Despondent — deeply discouraged with hope lost, caught between mere disappointment and total despair. (our word)

Build the Word

despondēre Latin: 'to give up, lose heart' (de- = away, spondere = to pledge or promise)

The root 'spondere' meant to make a solemn promise; 'despondent' literally means to break that promise to yourself — to surrender hope.

In the divorce hearing, the respondent calmly answered every accusation the plaintiff's lawyer raised against him.

Same root, new word — what does respondent mean here?

a person who lives permanently in a particular place

Not quite — This is the sense of 'resident,' unrelated to pledging or answering.

the party who answers or replies, especially the defending side in a legal case

Correct — 'respondent' comes from Latin respondēre (re- 'back' + spondēre 'to pledge/promise'); shares the spondēre root as despondent (de- 'away' + spondēre).

an official who keeps order and presides over a courtroom

Not quite — Plausible courtroom-context guess but unrelated to the root idea of pledging or promising in return.

The root connection →

The root despondēre splits into de- ('away') plus spondēre ('to pledge'), so despondent literally means to have pledged your hope away. Respondent keeps that very same spondēre root but swaps the prefix for re- ('back'), making it one who pledges an answer back to the charges against them.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / literary

Despondent is the deep discouragement of a broken spirit. Reach for it when someone has lost hope, not just faced a setback. For everyday disappointment or low motivation, 'discouraged' or 'disappointed' is simpler.
