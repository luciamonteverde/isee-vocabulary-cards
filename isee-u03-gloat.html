Gloat

/gloht/ — rhymes with float · verb

Hear It
What It Means

To gloat is to show smug, excessive pride or satisfaction about your own success or someone else's failure, in a way that irritates others.

Show it in context

After winning the bet, Tom couldn't resist gloating about it all week.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when gloat is the exact word.

Worked Example

At the chess tournament, the young grandmaster stood over his defeated opponent, smirking and narrating every brilliant move he'd made, making sure everyone in the room heard.

Gloat captures not just satisfaction but the SMUG, IRRITATING quality of rubbing it in. Simple 'satisfaction' wouldn't convey the annoying self-satisfaction and the showing-off of it.

Your Turn

After Sarah got the lead role in the play, she smiled broadly and told her best friend about it.

Is gloat the right word here?

No

Correct — 'No' is right: gloat means smug, showy delight aimed at annoying others, and sharing happy news warmly with a friend is genuine joy, not a wicked grin at anyone's expense.

Yes

Not quite — Choosing 'yes' assumes Sarah is showing off to needle others, but she simply smiled and told a friend her good news, with no sign of smug rubbing-it-in.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
boast

To brag about yourself, but it doesn't necessarily imply the cruel satisfaction in someone else's failure that gloat does.

swagger

Confident, proud behavior or movement, but it's more about attitude than rubbing success in someone's face.

relish

To enjoy deeply, but it can apply to any pleasure, whereas gloat specifically means malicious satisfaction.

Antonyms · opposites

humility — Modesty and lack of pride — the opposite of the arrogant, smug attitude gloat represents.

Build the Word

gloðra Old Norse: 'to grin wickedly, to stare mockingly'

The image is a wicked grin — you're so pleased with yourself that you can't help but smile smugly at others' expense.

He gave a sly grin as he revealed the surprise party.

Same root, new word — what does grin mean here?

a wide smile, here a sly or knowing one

Correct — Correct. Gloat traces to glotta, 'to grin wickedly,' and grin names that same wide expression, here a sly one hinting at a secret.

a loud, angry shout

Not quite — This is tempting because slyness can feel hostile, but the glotta root is about grinning, a facial expression, not shouting.

a sudden burst of tears

Not quite — This is tempting because strong feeling is involved, but glotta points to a wide grin, the opposite of crying.

The root connection →

Gloat and grin both point back to the old sense captured in glotta, 'to grin': gloating is grinning smugly at someone's expense, while a grin is the wide smile itself. Gloat's exact origin is debated by etymologists, so treat this as a shared root ancestor for grinning rather than a certain one-to-one link.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

informal

Gloat is too pointed and mean-spirited for polite company — it's the word for sneering satisfaction, especially when you're happy about someone else's troubles. For simple personal pleasure, just say 'pleased' or 'proud.'
