Disdain

/dis-DAYN/ — rhymes with rain · noun

Hear It
What It Means

Disdain is a feeling of strong contempt or scorn toward someone or something you consider beneath you.

Show it in context

She looked at the messy desk with obvious disdain.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when disdain is the exact word.

Worked Example

The wealthy collector glanced at the counterfeit painting with obvious disdain, waving it away without inspecting it closely.

Disdain captures not just disapproval or dislike, but the sense that the painting is beneath the collector's attention and status. The collector doesn't merely dislike it; she considers it unworthy of respect — that mix of contempt and superiority is what disdain names.

Your Turn

A girl frowns at her classmate's painting because the perspective is technically incorrect.

Is disdain the right word to describe how she feels?

Critical

Correct — She is simply disapproving of the painting's technical flaws, which is being critical, not showing contempt for the person.

Disdain

Not quite — Disdain would require looking down on her classmate as inferior or beneath her, not merely finding fault with the work.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
disrespect

Lack of respect. Disdain is stronger and includes the feeling that someone is beneath you.

arrogance

Excessive pride in yourself. Disdain is the contempt you feel toward others; arrogance is pride in yourself.

condescension

A patronizing attitude toward inferiors. Disdain is the inner feeling of contempt; condescension is how you show it.

Antonyms · opposites

respect — Valuing someone's worth and dignity. Disdain views them as beneath you; respect honors their value.

How Strong Is It?
dislike→disapproval→contempt→disdain

Disdain — contempt mixed with a sense of superiority, an unwillingness to consider the other person or thing worthy of your respect. (our word)

Build the Word

dignare Latin: 'to judge worthy'

Disdain comes from 'dis-' (not) plus a root meaning 'worthy.' It shares the same root family as deign (to consider worthy enough to do). Disdain is the opposite — to refuse to deign, to consider something unworthy of respect.

After years of cruelty, the tyrant finally met a condign fate: he was stripped of his throne and left with exactly what his crimes had earned him.

Same root, new word — what does condign mean here?

delivered suddenly and without any warning

Not quite — Sudden timing could describe a punishment, but that is about when it lands, not whether it is deserved. The Latin root dignus means 'worthy,' and condign is about a fate being worthy of the crime, not about surprise.

(of a punishment or reward) fitting and well-deserved

Correct — You traced the Latin root dignus ('worthy') from disdain into condign. Disdain is judging something not worthy, and a condign fate is one judged fully worthy of - fitting - what the tyrant's crimes had earned.

unusually harsh, cruel, or excessive

Not quite — A harsh punishment fits a tyrant's downfall, but condign means deserved and proportionate, not severe. The root dignus ('worthy') points to what fits a person's actions, not to how cruel the fate is.

The root connection →

Disdain and condign both come from the Latin root dignus, 'worthy' (the same root as deign). Disdain is treating something as not worthy of respect, while a condign fate is one that is fully worthy of, and a perfect fit for, what someone has done.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / literary

Disdain is a refined, somewhat dramatic word for a very specific kind of contempt — one that comes from feeling superior. Use it when you want to show that someone looks down on something as beneath them, not just that they dislike it. For mild disapproval, 'disapproval' works better; for pure contempt without the superiority angle, 'contempt' alone works.
