Impartial

/im-PAR-shul/ — rhymes with marshal · adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Someone who is impartial does not favor one side over another, instead making judgments based on facts rather than personal feelings.

Show it in context

An impartial jury listens to both sides before deciding.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when impartial is the exact word.

Worked Example

When judging the art competition, the teacher carefully evaluated each piece based on technique and creativity, setting aside her personal taste in art so every student had a fair chance.

Impartial is more precise than 'fair' because it specifically describes judging based on facts and merit rather than personal feelings or bias. It emphasizes the method—facts, not feelings—not just the outcome of equal treatment.

Your Turn

When two students disagreed about the best way to solve the math problem, the teacher didn't take sides.

Is impartial the best word for the teacher's role?

Yes

Not quite — Impartial emphasizes judging based on facts and merit. The teacher isn't weighing the answers here; she's simply declining to pick a side.

No

Correct — Neutral fits better. The teacher is choosing not to take sides in the disagreement, which is what neutral means; impartial would stress weighing the facts.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
fair

Treating people equally—but fair doesn't specifically emphasize the 'facts not feelings' method that impartial does.

objective

Based on facts rather than emotions—but objective is broader and doesn't emphasize the 'not favoring either side' comparison that impartial focuses on.

just

Treating with fairness and right judgment—but just emphasizes rightness and morality, not the specific method of facts over personal feeling.

Antonyms · opposites

biased — Showing favor based on personal preference—the opposite of impartial's facts-based fairness.

Build the Word

pars Latin: 'part, portion, or side'

Impartial comes from Latin for 'part'—someone impartial doesn't favor one part or side of a dispute, treating all sides equally on the basis of facts.

The company installed a tall glass partition to divide the large room into two separate work areas.

Same root, new word — what does partition mean here?

a wide doorway connecting two rooms together

Not quite — A doorway joins spaces together, but the sentence describes something that divides one room. The root 'pars' means 'part or portion,' so the answer should split a space into parts, not connect them.

a decorative covering hung across a window

Not quite — That describes a curtain, not something that splits a room into separate parts. The root 'pars' means 'part or portion,' pointing toward a divider that creates parts rather than a window covering.

a wall or screen that divides a space into parts

Correct — The root 'pars' means 'part, portion, or side,' the same root that makes an impartial judge refuse to favor one side. A partition carries that 'part' directly: it is a wall or screen that divides a space into parts, exactly what splits the room in the sentence.

The root connection →

Impartial comes from Latin 'pars,' meaning 'part or side,' so an impartial person favors no single part. A partition is built from that same root: it is the wall or screen that divides one space into separate parts.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal

Reach for impartial when describing judges, juries, or anyone making an important judgment where facts and merit matter more than personal feelings or loyalty. It's stronger and more specific than 'fair,' which just means treating people equally. Impartial emphasizes the method: judging by facts, not bias.
