Prerogative

prih-ROG-uh-tiv -- stress on ROG; note the first syllable sounds like 'prih,' not 'pree'. noun

Hear It
What It Means

Prerogative is a right or privilege belonging exclusively to a particular person, office, or class.

Show it in context

Granting pardons is the governor's prerogative, a power the state constitution gives to no one else.

See How It Works · a worked example, then try one yourself
Worked Example

Under the orchestra's bylaws, only the conductor may choose the season's final program; the musicians may suggest pieces, but the last word belongs to her office alone.

That exclusive right, attached to a particular office, is a prerogative. Choosing the program is the conductor's prerogative -- a privilege that belongs to her role and to no one else's.

Prerogative comes from Latin praerogativa -- literally 'asked before.' In ancient Rome, the praerogativa was the group of voters asked for its opinion first, before everyone else. From that head start grew the modern meaning: a special right that belongs to one office or class ahead of all others.

Use the root in a new word: interrogate

The detectives took turns interrogating the suspect about the missing paintings.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

Prerogative names a right that belongs exclusively to a particular role, office, or class: the crown's prerogative, a parent's prerogative, the editor's prerogative. In casual speech it gets watered down to mean any personal choice -- in formal writing and on the ISEE, keep it tied to a right that comes with a position.
