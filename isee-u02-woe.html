What It Means

Woe is a strong feeling of sadness or distress.

Here's how to decide when woe is the exact word.

Worked Example

A small business owner describes her past year: supply chain failures left her warehouse empty, a key employee quit unexpectedly, and a data breach cost her thousands. She calls it her 'year of woe.'

The situation isn't a single sad event—it's a cascade of troubles all hitting together. 'Woe' is more precise than plain 'sadness' because it captures that compounded, stacked-up suffering, as if telling a story of disasters. Sadness alone doesn't convey that narrative weight.

Your Turn

A student feels unhappy after getting a B on one quiz.

Would 'woe' be the right word here, or should we just say 'disappointment'?

disappointment

Correct — A single quiz grade is a brief, small letdown, which is exactly what 'disappointment' names.

woe

Not quite — 'Woe' means serious, deep troubles piled together, and one B on one quiz doesn't come close.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
sadness

Any feeling of being unhappy. Woe is deeper and often involves a story of multiple troubles.

sorrow

Deep, poetic sadness. Sorrow is quieter and more personal; woe suggests public trials or calamities.

anguish

Sharp, acute suffering. Anguish is sharp and immediate; woe is the heavy, accumulated weight of troubles.

Antonyms · opposites

joy — Happiness and delight — the opposite of the deep sadness woe names.

Build the Word

wā Old English / Germanic: 'distress, pain'

The root connects to ancient words for cries and laments — woe is the kind of sadness that makes you cry out.

At the memorial service, the grieving villagers began to bewail their fallen leader, their cries of anguish echoing through the hall.

Same root, new word — what does bewail mean here?

to travel a long distance on foot

Not quite — Plausible as a stray guess but unconnected to the root's sense of pain and crying out.

to cry out in deep grief or sorrow over something

Correct — bewail = intensive be- + 'wail'; 'wail' comes from Old Norse 'væla' / 'vei' meaning distress, the same *wai- interjection that gives 'woe' — a genuine shared-root cousin.

to gather together in a large, noisy crowd

Not quite — Tempting from the crowd imagery, but it ignores the grief/crying-out meaning carried by the root.

The root connection →

Both trace back to the ancient distress-cry captured in the root wā: woe is that outcry of pain named directly, while to bewail is to voice that same anguish aloud over a loss. They are historically related cousins rather than a clean look-alike pair, since the 'wail' in bewail reached English by way of Old Norse.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / literary

Woe is grand and serious—the sadness of kings or a tragic hero. Reach for it when troubles pile up and the suffering is deep and ongoing. For everyday unhappiness, 'sadness' is simpler and better.
