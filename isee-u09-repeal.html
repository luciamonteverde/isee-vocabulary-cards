Repeal

/rih-PEEL/ — rhymes with squeal · verb

Hear It
What It Means

To repeal a law or policy is to officially cancel it, ending its legal effect.

Show it in context

Lawmakers voted to repeal the outdated curfew law.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when repeal is the exact word.

Worked Example

After mounting public outcry over its unfairness, Congress moved to repeal the decades-old tax that had burdened small businesses.

The word repeal is precise here because it captures the formal, official process of erasing a law's legal effect entirely—not just modifying one part, but voiding the whole law. 'Cancel' is too vague; repeal specifically means ending legislation with legal power.

Your Turn

The restaurant owner decided to repeal the old menu item that no one was ordering anymore.

Is repeal the precise word here, or should it just say something else?

discontinue

Correct — Repeal applies only to laws and official policies. A restaurant dropping a menu item is an everyday business decision, so 'discontinue' is the precise, plainer word.

repeal

Not quite — 'Repeal' means to formally revoke a law or official policy, not to stop offering a menu item. Using it for a restaurant's ordinary choice overstates what is happening.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
cancel

Any stopping of something. Repeal is the formal, legal version with official power.

overturn

To reverse a court decision or ruling. Doesn't specifically mean erasing a law like repeal does.

reverse

To turn something back. Less formal and less specific to law than repeal.

Antonyms · opposites

enact — To officially put a law into effect—the opposite of repealing it.

How Strong Is It?
modify→amend→overturn→repeal

Repeal — the strongest: completely erase a law's legal effect, ending it entirely. (our word)

Build the Word

re- + appeler Old French: 'to call back, revoke'

A law is 'called back' or summoned away into nonexistence by lawmakers.

Citizens filed an appeal to ask the court to reconsider its ruling.

Same root, new word — what does appeal mean here?

a formal request calling on a higher court to reconsider its decision

Correct — Both repeal and appeal grow from the Old French root appeler, 'to call.' Repeal 'calls a law back' to cancel it, while an appeal 'calls upon' a court to reconsider.

a strong sense of attraction or charm

Not quite — This is a real everyday meaning of 'appeal,' but the root appeler means 'to call,' and in a courtroom an appeal is a call for the court to reconsider, not something charming.

a punishment handed down by a judge

Not quite — The courtroom setting makes this tempting, but appeler is about calling upon or calling back, not about penalties. An appeal calls for reconsideration; it is not the punishment itself.

The root connection →

The root appeler ('to call') links the two words: to repeal is to call a law back out of existence, and to appeal is to call upon a higher authority for a fresh decision.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal

Repeal is the formal, legal term—reach for it when discussing the official cancellation of laws or policies. For canceling dinner plans or removing an item from a menu, use the plainer 'cancel.'
