Detrimental

/dih-TRIH-men-tul/ — stress on second syllable; rhymes with mental · adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Detrimental describes something that causes harm or damage, especially over time.

Show it in context

Skipping breakfast can be detrimental to your concentration.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when detrimental is the exact word.

Worked Example

A high school student stops exercising, starts eating fast food every day, and sleeps only four hours a night. Within a few weeks, she notices her energy has crashed, her mood has darkened, and her grades have dropped. She realizes her lifestyle choices have been detrimental to her health and her success in school.

Detrimental is precise here because it captures a key meaning: harm that accumulates over time through small, repeated actions. The student's choices didn't cause immediate disaster but rather caused gradual damage—which is exactly what detrimental names.

Your Turn

A goalkeeper takes one kick to the face during a soccer match and gets a concussion.

Would you describe this injury as detrimental to her performance?

detrimental

Not quite — One kick and one concussion is a single sudden blow, but detrimental stresses damage that builds up gradually, so it does not fit a one-moment injury.

harmful

Correct — The concussion hurts her performance immediately, and harmful (or injurious) captures that instant damage far better than the slow-building word detrimental.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
harmful

Broader term for any kind of damage; detrimental specifies slow, cumulative harm.

damaging

Similar in meaning; detrimental adds the sense of gradual effect over time.

Formal word meaning working against or unfavorable; detrimental emphasizes time-based damage.

Antonyms · opposites

beneficial — Helpful and advantageous; opposite of detrimental's gradual harm.

Build the Word

detritus Latin: 'worn away or rubbed down'

Detrimental comes from a Latin word meaning 'worn away'—it captures how harm works slowly, rubbing away at your health or success over time.

The long war of attrition slowly drained the army's strength and resources.

Same root, new word — what does attrition mean here?

a sudden, total collapse of an army's forces

Not quite — The Latin terere means to rub or wear, so attrition is a slow wearing down, not a sudden collapse.

gradual wearing away through friction or repeated loss

Correct — Correct. Attrition shares the Latin terere (to rub) root family with detrimental's detritus, and it names a slow wearing away.

The root connection →

Detrimental (from detritus, worn away) and attrition both trace back to the Latin terere, to rub. Both describe things worn down little by little.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

neutral to formal

Detrimental is the precise word for harm that builds up over time through repeated action or exposure—not a single blow, but gradual damage. Reach for it when describing something that wears away at you slowly. For immediate, sudden damage, 'harmful' is simpler and more direct.
