Fallacy

/FAL-uh-see/ — stress on first syllable, rhymes with gallery · noun

Hear It
What It Means

A fallacy is a widely believed idea that is actually false or based on flawed reasoning.

Show it in context

The idea that lightning never strikes twice is a well-known fallacy.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when fallacy is the exact word.

Worked Example

Most medieval people believed that the sun orbited the earth, accepting an astronomical fallacy that dominated thought for centuries.

Fallacy is more precise than 'mistake' because it emphasizes a belief that is widely accepted despite being false or based on faulty reasoning. A mistake is personal error; a fallacy is deeply embedded in common (but wrong) knowledge.

Your Turn

A student studied the wrong chapter and failed the quiz because of her error.

Is this a fallacy?

Yes

Not quite — A fallacy is a false belief that misleads many people, not one person's slip-up. Studying the wrong chapter is a single student's mistake.

No

Correct — This is a personal error in what she did, not a widely shared false belief. Fallacy names a flawed idea that deceives people in general, which does not fit here.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
myth

A widely believed story, often legendary or cultural. Myth may be narrative-based; fallacy is rooted in faulty reasoning.

misconception

A false belief someone holds. Misconception doesn't emphasize how widely shared the false idea is or that it's based on logical error.

falsehood

Something that is simply not true. Falsehood doesn't capture the sense that many people believe a false idea despite lack of evidence.

Antonyms · opposites

fact — Something known or proven to be true; the opposite of a widely believed false idea.

Fallacy — a widespread false belief accepted by many, often rooted in flawed reasoning, not merely a personal error. (our word)

Build the Word

fallacia Latin: 'deception, deceit' (from fallere = 'to deceive, to go astray')

In Latin, fallere meant to deceive or lead someone wrong. A fallacy is a belief that 'leads us wrong'—it deceives many people by being false but widely accepted.

The rumor about the celebrity was completely false and hurt her reputation.

Same root, new word — what does false mean here?

not true, not corresponding to reality, and so misleading to those who believe it (from fallere 'to deceive')

Correct — Both trace to Latin fallere, 'to deceive.' A fallacy deceives many with a wrong belief, and a false rumor deceives by stating what is not true.

unkind or cruel in tone

Not quite — Tempting because the rumor hurt her, but false comes from fallere ('to deceive') and points to untruth, not cruelty. A statement can be false without being harsh.

spread widely and quickly

Not quite — Tempting because rumors travel fast, but false describes the truth value of the claim, from fallere ('to deceive'), not how far it spreads.

The root connection →

Fallacy and false both grow from Latin fallere, 'to deceive or lead astray.' Whatever is false leads you away from the truth, and a fallacy does the same on a larger scale by fooling many believers at once.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / neutral

Fallacy is the precise word when you want to name a widespread false belief that many people accept despite weak reasoning. For a single person's false belief, use 'misconception.' For a simple untruth, 'false' or 'lie' works better.
