Detain

/dih-TAYN/ — rhymes with rain · verb

Hear It
What It Means

To detain someone is to hold them back or keep them in official custody, often to stop them from leaving.

Show it in context

Guards detained the suspect at the gate until the police arrived.

Here's how to decide when detain is the exact word.

Worked Example

The border patrol detained the travelers for questioning when their documents appeared suspicious.

Detain is more specific than 'stop' or 'hold back' because it implies official custody or authority — forcibly keeping someone from leaving, typically by authorities or security forces.

Your Turn

A store manager held a customer's bag while they tried on clothes.

Is detain the precise word here, or should it say something else?

detain

Not quite — 'Detain' means an authority forcibly stops a person from leaving, usually in custody, but here only a bag was being kept, not a person restrained.

hold

Correct — The manager was simply keeping a bag while the customer tried on clothes, which is all 'hold' claims. No person was being kept from leaving.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
hold

Keep in place physically; lacks the official authority and control that detain implies.

arrest

Official custody following formal charges; detain can be temporary questioning without formal arrest.

confine

Keep in a restricted space or place; detain emphasizes preventing departure rather than restricting movement in an area.

Antonyms · opposites

release — Let someone go free — the opposite of detaining them.

Build the Word

tenere Latin: "to hold, keep"

Detain comes from Latin tenere, to hold — when authorities detain you, they hold you away from where you want to be.

She had a tenacious grip on her beliefs despite opposition.

Same root, new word — what does tenacious mean here?

holding on firmly and refusing to give up

Correct — Correct. Detain and tenacious both come from the Latin root tenere, 'to hold' or 'keep,' so a tenacious grip is one that holds on and will not release.

changing quickly to match the situation

Not quite — The mention of opposition can make flexibility sound plausible, but the root tenere means 'to hold,' which is the opposite of letting go or shifting.

openly hostile toward opponents

Not quite — 'Despite opposition' hints at conflict, but tenere means 'to hold, keep,' so tenacious is about holding on firmly, not about hostility.

The root connection →

The link between detain and tenacious is the Latin root tenere, 'to hold, keep.' Authorities who detain you hold you in place, and a tenacious person holds tightly to a belief or goal.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

neutral / formal

Detain is the official term for holding someone in custody or preventing their departure, usually by police or security. For simply holding or keeping something, use 'hold.' For locking someone away long-term, 'imprison' is more precise than detain.
