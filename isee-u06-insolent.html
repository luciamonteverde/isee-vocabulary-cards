Insolent

/IN-suh-lent/ — stress on first syllable; rhymes with tolerant · adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Insolent describes behavior that is boldly rude and disrespectful, especially toward someone who is owed respect.

Show it in context

The insolent recruit rolled his eyes while the sergeant was speaking.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when insolent is the exact word.

Worked Example

A junior officer glares at her commanding general, rolls her eyes, and flatly contradicts his order in front of the assembled troops.

The officer's behavior is not just rude — it is boldly defiant toward someone she is supposed to respect and obey. Insolent captures that combination: open disrespect aimed deliberately at an authority figure. A plain word like 'rude' would miss the confrontational boldness and the violation of the power dynamic.

Your Turn

A student avoids making eye contact with her principal and gives short, quiet answers to his questions.

Is insolent the precise word here, or should it say something else?

evasive

Correct — The student avoids eye contact and gives short, quiet answers. She is being withdrawn and evasive, dodging the exchange rather than confronting it.

insolent

Not quite — Insolent requires an open show of disrespect or bold rudeness. Avoiding eye contact and shrinking back is passive and withdrawn, the opposite of that boldness.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
rude

General impolite behavior—could be accidental or thoughtless. Insolent adds boldness and deliberate disrespect toward authority.

disrespectful

Shows lack of respect, but may be subtle or indirect. Insolent emphasizes open, confrontational boldness.

flippant

Treating something seriously with casual dismissiveness. Insolent adds confrontation and challenge to authority.

Antonyms · opposites

deferential — Showing proper respect and regard for authority—the opposite of insolent defiance.

Insolent — boldly disrespectful to authority; stronger than plain rudeness but stops short of outright scorn. (our word)

Build the Word

insolens (in- + solens) Latin: 'in-' (not) + 'solens' (accustomed, customary)

Insolent behavior throws off all rules of proper conduct — it is audaciously, unusually rude.

The old software became obsolete once the faster version was released.

Same root, new word — what does obsolete mean here?

brand-new and up to date

Not quite — Obsolete shares the sol- root from solere ('to be accustomed'); it means something has fallen out of customary use, the opposite of new.

widely used and in high demand

Not quite — This is tempting, but obsolete describes what people have stopped using, not what is popular; its prefix ob- shifts the 'accustomed' root toward 'no longer accustomed.'

no longer in use; out of date

Correct — Insolent and obsolete share the Latin root sol- from solere, 'to be accustomed.' Insolent behavior is not accustomed to proper manners, and something obsolete is no longer part of accustomed, everyday use.

The root connection →

The bridge is solere, 'to be accustomed': insolens joins in- ('not') to it for behavior that breaks with custom, while obsolete joins ob- to it for a thing that has dropped out of customary use.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

neutral / standard

Insolent is stronger than rude because it emphasizes boldness and direct disrespect toward someone owed respect. Use it when someone openly defies or challenges authority. For mild, everyday rudeness, rude is simpler and better.
