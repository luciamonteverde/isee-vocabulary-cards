Intimidate

/in-TIM-uh-dayt/ — stress on second syllable · verb

Hear It
What It Means

To intimidate someone is to frighten or unsettle them, often to make them back down or obey.

Show it in context

The team's huge defenders intimidate opponents before the game even starts.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when intimidate is the exact word.

Worked Example

A boxing champion enters the ring early, staring down her nervous opponent across the canvas, rolling her shoulders and glowering—clearly using her size and presence to intimidate.

The champion's goal is to make her opponent feel unsettled and frightened so they lose confidence. That calculated attempt to frighten someone into a weaker position—that's exactly what intimidate means. 'Scare' alone doesn't capture the deliberate intent to control or dominate.

Your Turn

A sudden thunderstorm rolls across the sky, with lightning and booming thunder.

Would you say the storm intimidates the villagers?

No

Correct — The storm frightens or terrifies the villagers, but it cannot intimidate them. Intimidating requires an intent to make someone submit, and a thunderstorm has no purpose behind it.

Yes

Not quite — 'Yes' is tempting because the storm clearly scares the villagers, but intimidate means deliberately frightening someone into obeying or backing down. A storm has no will or goal, so it can frighten but not intimidate.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
frighten

Simply to cause fear. Intimidate adds purpose: the fear is meant to make someone back down or obey.

bully

Repeated harassment to dominate someone. Intimidate is a single act of making someone afraid; bullying is sustained.

threaten

To warn of harm directly. Intimidate works through fear and presence; threat uses explicit language.

Antonyms · opposites

encourage — To give someone confidence and hope—the opposite of the fear and doubt that intimidate creates.

How Strong Is It?
frighten→intimidate→threaten→terrorize

Intimidate—deliberate fear aimed at control, stronger than simple fright but less extreme than violence or terror. (our word)

Build the Word

timidus Latin: fearful, timid, lacking courage

To intimidate someone is to make them timid—to fill them with the hesitation and fear of someone afraid to act.

The timid child whispered her answer rather than speaking aloud to the class.

Same root, new word — what does timid mean here?

loud and eager for attention

Not quite — The child does draw a bit of notice, which makes this tempting, but the root timidus means 'fearful, timid.' Timid points to fear and shyness, the opposite of being loud and attention-seeking.

fearful, shy, lacking boldness

Correct — Intimidate and timid share the Latin root timidus, 'fearful, lacking courage.' To intimidate is to make someone timid, and the timid child shows that fearful, boldness-lacking state in her whisper.

careful to follow every rule

Not quite — A quiet child can look obedient, so this seems close, but timidus ('fearful, lacking courage') is about being afraid to act boldly, not about following rules.

The root connection →

The root timidus ('fearful, timid') is the link: timid names the fearful state directly, while intimidate means to fill someone with that same fear so they hold back or give in.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

neutral / conversational

Intimidate is the right word when someone deliberately tries to make another person afraid in order to control them or make them back down. For plain fear without the intent, use 'frighten' or 'scare.' For repeated, cruel behavior, 'bully' is more precise.
