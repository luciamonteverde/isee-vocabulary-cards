Temper

/TEM-per/ — rhymes with remember · noun

Hear It
What It Means

A person's temper is their tendency to become angry or lose control of their emotions quickly.

Show it in context

He lost his temper when the referee made an unfair call.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when temper is the exact word.

Worked Example

A coach is known for shouting at players, slamming clipboards, and nearly getting ejected from games whenever calls go against his team.

Temper captures not just the coach's anger but his characteristic quickness to lose emotional control. We're seeing his tendency to explode — that's his nature. Temper means this is who he is, not just a one-time angry outburst. That's more precise than simply saying 'his anger.'

Your Turn

A usually calm student gets frustrated when a test is harder than expected and sighs loudly.

Does the student's bad temper cause the sigh, or is this just momentary frustration?

Frustration

Correct — This is just momentary frustration — a passing moment of irritation at an unexpectedly hard test, not a habitual failure of self-control.

Temper

Not quite — Temper implies a characteristic, ongoing tendency to lose control, but this normally calm student is showing one reaction, not an established bad temper.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
Anger

Any feeling of being mad. Temper is the characteristic, ongoing tendency to become angry easily and lose control.

Irritability

Being easily annoyed. Temper includes actual explosive loss of emotional control, not just persistent annoyance.

Volatility

Being unpredictable and prone to emotional swings. Temper is the specific tendency to lose control when angry; volatility is broader emotional instability.

Antonyms · opposites

Composure — The ability to stay calm and in control. Someone with a good temper maintains composure; someone who loses their temper abandons it.

Build the Word

temperare Latin: 'to moderate, blend, regulate'

The word originally meant to mix or blend things in the right proportions — hence, a person with a balanced temper has emotions properly regulated, while someone who loses their temper has let emotions get dangerously out of balance.

Known for his temperance, the monk ate only modest portions and refused every luxury, keeping his appetites carefully in balance.

Same root, new word — what does temperance mean here?

moderation and self-restraint, especially over one's appetites and habits

Correct — You traced the Latin root temperare ('to moderate, blend, regulate') straight from temper into temperance. A well-tempered person keeps emotions balanced, and the monk's temperance keeps his appetites balanced, which is the same regulating root at work.

a sudden outburst of fierce, uncontrolled anger

Not quite — It is natural to link temperance back to the headword temper and picture an angry outburst, but the Latin root temperare means 'to moderate, blend, regulate.' A burst of fury is the loss of that balance, not the root's meaning.

boldness and an eagerness to take big risks

Not quite — Boldness sounds like a personality trait you could guess at, but the root temperare means 'to moderate, blend, regulate.' Taking big risks has nothing to do with holding things in careful, balanced proportion.

The root connection →

Temper and temperance both come from the Latin root temperare, 'to moderate, blend, regulate.' Temper is your emotions kept in the right proportion, and temperance is your appetites and habits kept in the right proportion.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

neutral / everyday

Temper describes a characteristic tendency to lose emotional control, while anger describes the feeling itself. When someone 'has a bad temper,' you're describing their nature; when they 'feel anger,' you're describing a momentary emotion. Use temper when talking about a person's emotional pattern or how they typically respond when provoked.
