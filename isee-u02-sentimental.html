Sentimental

/sen-tuh-MEN-tul/ — stress on third syllable · adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Sentimental describes feelings, memories, or objects that stir up tenderness, nostalgia, or emotional attachment.

Show it in context

He kept the broken watch for sentimental reasons — it had belonged to his grandfather.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when sentimental is the exact word.

Worked Example

An elderly woman opens a box of letters written by her late husband during their courtship, and even though decades have passed, she reads through them with tears and a soft smile.

The word 'sentimental' captures not just that the letters matter to her, but that their value lies entirely in the emotions and tender memories they stir. Their worth is emotional and memory-bound, not monetary or practical. A plainer word like 'meaningful' wouldn't convey that specific emotional attachment to the past.

Your Turn

A teenager keeps all of her old school yearbooks in a box under her bed and looks at them sometimes when she is feeling down.

Is 'sentimental' the right word for why she keeps them?

Sentimental

Correct — Yes — she keeps the yearbooks for their emotional and memory value, an attachment to the people and experiences they represent, which is exactly what 'sentimental' means.

Practical

Not quite — No — she isn't keeping them for any useful or practical purpose; her attachment is purely emotional and tied to memory, not everyday usefulness.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
emotional

General word for stirring feelings. 'Sentimental' is more specific — tied to memory and objects from the past.

tender

Gentle, affectionate, soft. 'Sentimental' adds the element of memory or past attachment.

touching

Moving, emotionally affecting. 'Sentimental' focuses on the value of memory to the person, not just emotional impact.

Antonyms · opposites

detached — Objective and unemotional — the opposite of sentimental attachment based on feeling and memory.

How Strong Is It?
practical→emotional→affectionate→sentimental

Sentimental — the strongest of the group: deeply emotionally connected to memory and the past, valued for feeling rather than function. (our word)

Build the Word

sentire Latin: 'to feel, to perceive'

The word traces back to the Latin root meaning 'to feel' — sentimental things are valued for the feelings they stir, especially feelings tied to memory.

In the film, the space probe gradually becomes sentient, reacting with fear and curiosity as it begins to experience the world around it.

Same root, new word — what does sentient mean here?

moving very quickly and arriving without any warning

Not quite — Wrong-but-plausible sci-fi association; not derived from a 'to feel' root.

able to perceive things and experience feelings; consciously aware

Correct — 'sentient' derives from Latin 'sentientem', present participle of 'sentire' ('to feel, to perceive') — the same root behind 'sentimental'.

extremely large or vast in physical size

Not quite — A plausible space-scale guess, but unrelated to perception/feeling.

The root connection →

Both stem from the root sentire ('to feel, to perceive'): sentimental describes things we prize for the feelings they stir, while sentient describes a being able to feel and perceive at all. It is the same root turned from cherished emotion toward the capacity for raw awareness itself.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

neutral / everyday

Sentimental describes the emotional pull of objects or memories tied to the past. Use it when someone values something because of what it feels like to remember, not for practical use. In everyday speech, you might just say 'meaningful' or 'emotional,' but 'sentimental' is more precise about that memory-based, heart-tugging attachment.
