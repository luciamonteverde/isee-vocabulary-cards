Blatant

/BLAH-tunt/ — stress on first syllable, rhymes with tenant · adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Something blatant is so obvious and out in the open that it cannot be missed or reasonably denied.

Show it in context

The referee somehow missed the blatant push right in front of him.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when blatant is the exact word.

Worked Example

The student submitted an essay copied word-for-word from Wikipedia — an act of plagiarism so blatant that no one could deny it or explain it away.

Blatant captures not just that the plagiarism occurred, but that it was flagrant and obvious, done without any attempt to hide it. This is more precise than 'obvious' (which could describe subtle things) or 'dishonest' (which is broader and does not emphasize the brazen openness).

Your Turn

The slight tremor in her voice when she said 'I'm fine' suggested she was upset, though she was trying to hide it.

Is this a blatant display of emotion?

Yes

Not quite — Blatant means obvious and out in the open, impossible to miss. Her emotion was concealed and could only be read from subtle signs, so it was not blatant.

No

Correct — Her feeling was hidden; you had to notice a slight tremor to detect it. Blatant describes emotion that is loud and out in the open, not something you must search for.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
obvious

Something easy to see or understand. Blatant adds the sense of being unmissable and unashamed — flagrantly open.

glaring

Very noticeable and hard to overlook. Blatant emphasizes brazen openness and refusal to hide or deny.

flagrant

Shocking and morally offensive wrongdoing. Blatant focuses on visibility; flagrant focuses on the offense itself.

Antonyms · opposites

subtle — Delicate, difficult to notice, requiring careful attention — the opposite of blatant's unmissable openness.

Build the Word

uncertain origin, possibly from bleat English (coined by Spenser) — origin uncertain

Spenser created blatant to describe something so loud, obvious, and clamorous it cannot be ignored — like a bleating sheep you cannot miss.

The goat bleated loudly across the farm all morning.

Same root, new word — what does bleat mean here?

to make a soft, secret whisper

Not quite — Blatant means loud and impossible to ignore, and if it really does come from bleat, that word names a loud, insistent animal cry, the very opposite of a whisper.

to wander quietly while feeding

Not quite — This pictures a calm grazing animal, but the possible link to bleat points to the noisy sound the animal makes, not the way it moves.

to cry out loudly and repeatedly, like a sheep or goat

Correct — Blatant may come from bleat, the loud cry of a sheep or goat, though its origin is not certain. If that link holds, a bleating animal that will not stop captures exactly what blatant means: loud, obvious, and impossible to overlook.

The root connection →

The suggested source is the English word bleat: Spenser possibly coined blatant from that insistent animal noise, so both share the idea of a sound too loud to ignore, even though the origin stays uncertain.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

neutral / everyday

Blatant is best for something so obvious and out-in-the-open that it cannot be reasonably denied or overlooked. Use it when you want to emphasize how flagrantly visible something wrong is. For things that are merely easy to see, 'obvious' is simpler and sufficient.
