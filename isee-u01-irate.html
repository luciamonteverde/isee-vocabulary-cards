Irate

/eye-RAYT/ — stress the second syllable · adjective

Hear it

What it means

Irate describes someone who has been pushed to an extreme, seething level of anger.

Show it in context

The irate passengers complained loudly when their flight was delayed for the third time.

See how it works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when irate is the exact word.

Worked example

A customer returns to the repair shop with the same broken phone for the third time. His voice is shaking, and he demands to see the manager immediately.

This isn't ordinary irritation — he has been pushed past his limit by repeated provocation and is now boiling over. That provoked, seething state is exactly what irate names. Because the anger has built up to an extreme, irate is more precise than the plain word "angry."

Your Turn

A student sighs when his pencil tip breaks in the middle of class.

Is irate the precise word for how he feels, or should it just say "annoyed"?

Irate

Not quite — Irate means furiously, blazingly angry — far too strong for a student sighing over a broken pencil tip.

annoyed

Correct — The feeling is mild and momentary, and nothing has pushed him to an extreme.

Synonyms · words that mean nearly the same

open each word to see how it differs

furious

Wild, storming anger. Just as hot as irate, but irate hints that something specific provoked it.

livid

So angry it shows on your face. Like irate, it describes a person at boiling point.

incensed

Made furious by something unfair or insulting — the closest cousin of irate, and a touch more formal.

Antonyms · opposites

composed — calm and fully in control of your feelings — the state an irate person has completely lost.

Build the word

where it comes from

ira Latin: "anger"

Straight from Latin ira, anger — the same root as ire. Careful: it is not the prefix ir- (not) + "ate" — that decomposition is a false friend.

The irascible old landlord snapped and shouted at anyone who so much as knocked on his door.

Same root, new word — what does irascible mean here?

easily embarrassed and shy

Not quite — Shyness is about social awkwardness, but the Latin root ira behind irate and irascible means 'anger,' not embarrassment. The root points to a hot temper, not to a nervous blush.

slow, patient, and calm

Not quite — This reads like the opposite of an angry word, which is the trap. The Latin root ira means 'anger,' so irascible has to describe someone easily heated, not someone slow and calm.

quick to become angry; hot-tempered

Correct — You carried the Latin root ira ('anger') from irate into irascible. Add a suffix meaning 'prone to' and the root describes exactly the landlord who is quick to snap and shout at anyone.

The root connection →

Irate and irascible both come straight from the Latin root ira, 'anger' (the same root as ire). Irate means angry right now, while irascible means built to become angry easily. Careful: irascible is not ir- ('not') plus a stem, which is a false friend.

Word choice · its tone, and when to use it

reporting / formal

Irate is the word news reports and complaint letters reach for: irate customers, irate callers, irate letters to the editor. Use it for a person visibly boiling over at some provocation. For mild, everyday crossness, "annoyed" or "angry" is the simpler, better choice.
