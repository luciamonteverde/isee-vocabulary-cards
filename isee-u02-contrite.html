Contrite

/kən-TRYT/ — rhymes with incite · adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Someone who is contrite feels real guilt and sorrow for something wrong they did, and often wants to make up for it.

Show it in context

The contrite student apologized to the librarian and offered to repair the torn book.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when contrite is the exact word.

Worked Example

After a fight, the boy realized he'd hurt his best friend deeply. Truly ashamed, he showed up at her house with an apology written in his own hand, offered to help with her homework all summer to make amends, and meant every word — a contrite gesture born of genuine remorse.

Contrite is far more precise than just 'sorry.' It means not just regret, but deep, genuine guilt paired with a real desire to fix what you broke and make things right. Saying someone is contrite tells us their remorse is authentic and they're motivated to repair harm.

Your Turn

A student apologizes to the teacher only after learning that cheating will drop her grade from an A to a C.

Is the student being contrite, or is it something else?

Contrite

Not quite — Contrite means feeling genuine remorse and wanting to make amends, but she's sorry only about the consequences to herself, not out of real guilt.

Opportunistic

Correct — Opportunistic fits: she apologizes only because getting caught now costs her the grade, not from any genuine remorse.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
sorry

Regret for something, but doesn't necessarily mean deep remorse or a drive to repair harm — you can say 'sorry' without truly feeling contrite.

remorseful

Feeling regret or guilt, but doesn't capture contrite's insistence on genuine guilt AND the motivation to make things right.

penitent

Showing sorrow and willingness to repent, similar to contrite, but more often tied to spiritual or religious atonement rather than personal reparation.

Antonyms · opposites

unrepentant — Showing no remorse or regret for wrongdoing — the absolute opposite of the guilt and desire to amend that characterizes contrition.

Contrite — stronger than guilt alone: genuine sorrow for wrong committed, paired with a real desire to repair harm and make amends. (our word)

Build the Word

contritus Latin: 'worn down, crushed'

Contrite originally meant 'crushed' — as if guilt and remorse had ground a person down until they could do nothing but confess and try to make amends. The crushing weight of genuine remorse is the heart of contrition.

After the storm, the beach was covered in detritus — bits of shell, seaweed, and driftwood worn loose and left behind by the waves.

Same root, new word — what does detritus mean here?

a sudden, violent burst of rain or wind

Not quite — Plausible because the sentence opens with a storm, but detritus names leftover debris, not the weather event, and 'storm' has no link to the terere root.

a narrow path running along a shoreline

Not quite — Plausible from the beach setting, but describes a location rather than worn-away material.

loose fragments or debris left behind after something breaks down or wears away

Correct — Contrite = Latin 'conterere' (con- + terere 'to rub/grind/wear'), past participle 'contritus' = 'worn down, crushed.' Detritus = 'de-' + terere, past participle 'detritus' = 'worn away' — the same terere root and past-participle formation.

The root connection →

The root contritus is con- joined to terere ('to rub, grind, wear'), a past participle meaning 'worn down.' Detritus is formed the same way from de- plus that same terere, so a contrite person is someone ground down by remorse, just as detritus is matter ground away and left behind.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / somewhat literary

Contrite is stronger and more serious than 'sorry.' Use it when someone shows genuine, deep guilt and a clear drive to repair harm — not just polite regret or an apology made to escape trouble. Reserve it for true remorse and a sincere effort to make things right.
