Maneuver

/muh-NOO-ver/ — rhymes with mover · verb

Hear It
What It Means

To maneuver is to move or steer something with skill and careful control, especially in a tight or tricky space.

Show it in context

With one quick maneuver, the pilot slipped the plane between the storm cells.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when maneuver is the exact word.

Worked Example

The surgeon worked carefully through the narrow space, maneuvering the delicate instrument to extract the stone without damaging the surrounding tissue.

Maneuver precisely describes skillful movement in a constrained, difficult space. Plain words like 'move' fail to capture the expertise, careful control, and tight-space navigation required. Maneuver is exact because it emphasizes both the skill involved and the challenging conditions that make the action noteworthy.

Your Turn

The toddler ran down the wide hallway in a straight line toward the playground.

Is 'maneuver' the precise word here, or should it be something else?

run

Correct — Since the child just moves quickly in a straight line with no obstacles in the way, 'run' describes the action plainly and accurately.

maneuver

Not quite — The toddler is running straight down a wide, open hallway with nothing to steer around, so 'maneuver', which implies skilled steering through tight constraints, is far more than this simple dash needs.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
move

To change position or location generally, but lacks maneuver's emphasis on skill and navigation through tight or difficult constraints.

shift

To change position to a new spot, but without the implication of skillful control through tricky or constrained spaces.

steer

To direct a vehicle or course, but does not necessarily imply navigating through tight spaces or the obstacle-avoidance that maneuver suggests.

Antonyms · opposites

careen — To move forward rapidly and without proper control, the opposite of maneuver's careful, skilled, controlled movement.

Build the Word

manu operari Latin: to work by hand

The word originally meant 'work done by hand,' from Latin roots for 'hand' (manu) and 'to work' (operari). It evolved to mean skillfully handling and controlling something, like carefully moving a vehicle with hand and expertise.

The engineer manually adjusted the machine to ensure it worked correctly.

Same root, new word — what does manually mean here?

done by hand; operated directly by hand

Correct — Correct. 'Manually' shares the manus root ('hand') behind 'maneuver', so it means done directly by hand.

done quickly and automatically

Not quite — 'Manually' comes from the 'hand' root, so it means by hand, the opposite of automatic; 'quickly and automatically' misreads that root.

The root connection →

The Latin manus means 'hand' (from 'manu operari', to work by hand). To 'maneuver' is to handle or steer something skillfully, and to do a task 'manually' is to do it by hand; both come from that 'hand' root.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

neutral / formal

Maneuver is the right word when you are describing skilled, careful movement in a constrained or difficult space. It emphasizes both the expertise required and the tight conditions. For simple movement, use 'move.' For directing a vehicle in open space, 'steer' works. But when you need to convey skilled navigation through tight spaces or tricky situations, maneuver is more precise.
