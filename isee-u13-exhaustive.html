What It Means

Here's how to decide when exhaustive is the exact word.

Worked Example

After an exhaustive search of every drawer and pocket, she found the ring in her shoe.

The word exhaustive means leaving nothing out or covering every possible detail. In this sentence, she searched every single location—every drawer and every pocket—making the search truly exhaustive. A plainer word like "thorough" would work, but it doesn't emphasize as strongly that EVERY possibility was checked. Exhaustive specifies that nothing at all was overlooked.

Your Turn

She looked in a few rooms for her keys and eventually found them.

Would we call this an 'exhaustive search'?

search

Correct — She only checked a few rooms before finding the keys, so a plain 'search' is accurate. An exhaustive search would mean checking absolutely every possible location.

exhaustive search

Not quite — An exhaustive search means checking absolutely EVERY possible location, and she only looked in a few rooms before the keys turned up.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
thorough

Careful and detailed, but doesn't necessarily mean EVERY option was checked—just that the work was done carefully.

complete

All parts are included, but doesn't emphasize the effort or totality the way exhaustive does.

comprehensive

Covers a wide range, but exhaustive emphasizes checking EVERY detail, not just a broad range.

Antonyms · opposites

superficial — Shallow, only scratching the surface—the opposite of exhaustive's complete, thorough coverage.

Build the Word

exhaust Latin: ex- (out) + haurire (to draw/pull)

The root means to draw or pull something out completely until nothing is left—that's what exhaustive does: draws out every detail until nothing remains unchecked.

The intense practice will exhaust the pitcher's arm, using up all her strength.

Same root, new word — what does exhaust mean here?

to use up completely; to drain all of someone's strength or resources

Correct — Exhaustive and exhaust both come from Latin haurire, 'to draw out,' with ex- ('out'). An exhaustive search draws out every detail, and to exhaust an arm draws out every last bit of its strength.

to gently warm up

Not quite — Practice does warm up an arm, but the root (ex- 'out' plus haurire 'to draw') means drawing everything out until nothing is left, so exhaust means to drain, not to warm up.

to strengthen and build up

Not quite — This is the opposite result. Drawing something out completely leaves nothing behind, so exhaust depletes rather than builds.

The root connection →

The root idea is drawing something out until nothing remains. Exhaustive draws out every detail so none is missed, while exhausting a pitcher's arm draws out all her strength until it is empty.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / academic

Use exhaustive when you want to emphasize that EVERY detail or possibility was covered, leaving absolutely nothing out. It's more formal and emphatic than "thorough" and suggests a definitive, comprehensive treatment. For casual situations ("thorough cleaning"), just use "thorough."
