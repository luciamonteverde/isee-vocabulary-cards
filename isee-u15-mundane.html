Mundane

/mun-DAYN/ — rhymes with domain · adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Something that is mundane is very ordinary and unremarkable, part of everyday routine rather than exciting or unusual.

Show it in context

He enlivened mundane chores like dishwashing by inventing games.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when mundane is the exact word.

Worked Example

Every morning she followed the same mundane routine: alarm clock, shower, toast, bus stop, office desk—unchanged for three years.

These activities are ordinary and part of everyday life, not exciting or unusual. 'Ordinary' fits, but mundane more precisely suggests this is routine daily existence that lacks excitement or any sense of distinction.

Your Turn

Sarah's first day as a surgeon was nerve-wracking as she assisted on a complicated heart operation with an expert team.

Would mundane be a good word to describe Sarah's experience?

extraordinary

Correct — Her first day assisting on a complicated heart operation was intense and remarkable, so a word like 'extraordinary' fits the experience.

mundane

Not quite — 'Mundane' means dull, everyday routine. A nerve-wracking, high-stakes surgery demanding intense focus and skill is the opposite of ordinary.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
ordinary

Means 'average' or 'normal,' but lacks mundane's sense of tedious, repetitive routine and lack of excitement.

routine

Describes a repeated procedure or habit, but it's neutral—routine can be helpful or comforting. Mundane implies it's tedious and unexciting.

humdrum

Also means dull and repetitive, but lacks mundane's implication that something is simply part of everyday life rather than actively tiresome.

Antonyms · opposites

extraordinary — Unusual and noteworthy—the opposite of mundane's everyday, unremarkable nature.

Build the Word

mundus Latin: 'world'

Mundane comes from the Latin word for 'world.' Originally it meant 'worldly' or 'of this material world,' not spiritual or elevated. Over time, it came to mean ordinary, everyday, and thus dull or uninspiring—things of the everyday world.

The monks rejected worldly possessions in favor of spiritual meditation and prayer.

Same root, new word — what does worldly mean here?

extremely rare and expensive

Not quite — Tempting because giving up possessions can sound like a costly sacrifice, but worldly points to ordinary earthly things in general, not to how valuable they are.

relating to the material world and everyday life; not spiritual or otherworldly

Correct — Mundane comes from Latin mundus, 'world,' and worldly means much the same thing, but worldly is a native English word built from 'world,' a Germanic word, not from mundus. They are translation-equivalents that arrived at the same meaning in two different language families, not a shared root.

having to do with faith and prayer

Not quite — Tempting because the sentence mentions the spiritual, but worldly is the contrast to spiritual, naming everyday material life rather than religious devotion.

The root connection →

Here the bridge is meaning, not word history: Latin mundus (behind mundane) and English 'world' (behind worldly) both name the everyday physical world, yet they come from separate language families and are not related roots. Mundane and worldly are twins in sense only, which is why worldly still captures the same 'of this ordinary world' idea.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

neutral

Mundane is the word when you want to emphasize that something is unremarkably ordinary and lacks excitement as part of routine daily life. For something that's just average or normal, 'ordinary' works fine; for something actively dull or tedious, 'tedious' might be more precise.
