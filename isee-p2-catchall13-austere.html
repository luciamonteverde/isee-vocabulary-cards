Austere

aw-STEER -- from Greek austeros, 'harsh, dry, bitter,' first said of taste and later of people. adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Austere is severe or strict in manner, or stripped down to plain essentials.

Show it in context

The judge's austere expression gave no hint of what she was thinking.

See How It Works · a worked example, then try one yourself
Worked Example

A retired teacher lives in a single room with a cot, a lamp, one chair, and a shelf of books, and gives away anything she has not used in a year.

A life or a manner stripped down to strict, plain essentials is exactly what austere describes. Unlike 'poor,' which is forced on someone, austere often names a severity that a person chooses.

rootauster-harsh, bitter, dry (Greek (austeros), via Latin austerus)

Greek austeros first described a taste -- dry, bitter, and sharp on the tongue -- and Latin borrowed it as austerus. English carried the word from bitter flavors to bitter manners, so an austere person is 'harsh to the taste' in the way they treat others. The same root gives us austerity.
