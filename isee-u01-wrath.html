Wrath

/rath/ — rhymes with math · noun

Hear it

What it means

Wrath is intense anger, often with a desire to get back at whoever caused it.

Show it in context

The king's wrath was so great that his advisors feared even mentioning the failed harvest.

See how it works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when wrath is the exact word.

Worked example

A myth tells of a sea god who discovers that sailors broke their promise, and now gathers a storm to punish their whole fleet.

The anger here is enormous and aimed at payback — that vengeful, punishing anger is exactly what wrath names. Because revenge is the whole point of the sentence, wrath is more precise than the plain word "anger."

Your Turn

A neighbor is briefly annoyed when her delivery arrives an hour late.

Is wrath the precise word here, or should it just say "annoyance"?

annoyance

Correct — The feeling is mild and passing and nobody is planning payback — exactly what "annoyance" describes.

wrath

Not quite — A brief, mild irritation with no thought of payback makes wrath far too strong here.

Synonyms · words that mean nearly the same

open each word to see how it differs

anger

Any feeling of being mad. Wrath is the fierce, vengeful kind.

fury

Wild, explosive anger — as strong as wrath, but without wrath's flavor of punishment.

indignation

Anger at something unfair — righteous like wrath, but usually without the revenge.

Antonyms · opposites

serenity — complete calm and peace of mind — the state wrath destroys.

Build the word

where it comes from

wrāth Old English: "twisted, angry"

Same family as writhe (to twist and squirm) — wrath is anger so strong it twists you up inside.

Thick vines wreathed the old iron gate, twisting and coiling around every bar.

Same root, new word — what does wreathe mean here?

to decorate a hall with flowers and ribbons

Not quite — Decorating a hall sounds festive, and it is easy to jump from wreathe to a flowery 'wreath,' but that matches the noun's look, not its meaning. The Old English root behind wrath and wreathe means 'twist,' and hanging up decorations has nothing to do with twisting.

to twist, coil, or wind around something

Correct — The Old English 'twist' root that wrath shares with writhe is exactly what carries into wreathe, where the vines twist and coil around every bar. You matched the meaning of the root, not just a look-alike noun.

to slowly fade from view

Not quite — Slowly fading is a smooth motion word, but the Old English root wreathe shares with wrath means 'twist, coil,' not fade. Nothing in that twisting root points to something disappearing from view.

The root connection →

Wrath, writhe, and wreathe all grow from the same Old English root meaning 'twist.' Wrath is anger so strong it twists you up inside, while to wreathe is to twist and coil around an object, like the vines gripping the iron gate.

Word choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / literary

Wrath is grand, serious anger — the anger of kings, gods, and storms. Reach for it when the anger is powerful and threatens consequences. For everyday irritation, the plain word "anger" is the simpler, better choice.
