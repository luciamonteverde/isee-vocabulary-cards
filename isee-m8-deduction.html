Deduction

/dih-DUK-shun/ — stress on DUK · noun

REVIEW (Questions, Investigation & Reflection)

Hear It
What It Means

A deduction is a conclusion you reach by reasoning from evidence or clues rather than being told directly.

Show it in context

From the muddy boots by the door, Sherlock made his first deduction of the morning.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when deduction is the exact word.

Worked Example

By examining the muddy footprints in the entryway and the broken window latch, the investigator made her deduction: someone had forced their way inside.

de- (down from) + duct (Latin ducere, 'to lead') + -ion (act of) = the act of leading DOWN from specific clues (muddy prints, broken window) to a definite conclusion (forced entry after dark). This captures the logical method: moving from observable evidence down to a particular answer. The investigator reasons from the specific facts downward to the verdict.

Your Turn

Emma received her monthly paycheck and noticed a large deduction marked 'taxes' on the stub.

Does this deduction mean the same thing as the investigator's deduction about the break-in, or is there a meaning shift?

A meaning shift (subtraction of money, not reasoning)

Correct — Both uses build from de- (away from) + duct (lead), but the paycheck's 'deduction' means money led away from the total, a subtraction. The investigator's deduction means reasoning led down from clues to a conclusion. Same morphemes, but Emma's tax line is about numbers being taken out, not thinking, so the sense genuinely shifts.

Same meaning (a logical conclusion)

Not quite — It's tempting to assume one word always carries one meaning, but the tax line on Emma's stub isn't a reasoned conclusion about anything. It's an amount removed from her pay, so treating it as identical to the investigator's chain of reasoning misses how context flips the sense.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
conclusion

The endpoint of reasoning. Deduction emphasizes the logical process and reasoning from evidence leading to that endpoint.

inference

Drawing a conclusion from evidence. Similar to deduction, but inference is broader and doesn't always imply the formal logical reasoning that deduction does.

reasoning

The mental process itself. Reasoning is the activity; deduction is both the method and the conclusion that results from it.

Antonyms · opposites

assumption — A claim accepted without evidence. Deduction requires evidence and logical reasoning; an assumption is taken for granted.

Build the Word · the heart of this unit

de + duct + ion

Another DOUBLE decomposition: de- (down from) + duct (Latin ducere, 'to lead' — as in conduct, aqueduct) + -ion (act of) = the act of leading DOWN from a general rule to a specific conclusion. That's exactly what a detective does. Second real sense: money 'led away' from a total — a paycheck deduction. Same parts; context decides.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

neutral

Reach for deduction when describing a conclusion reached through logical reasoning from evidence or clues. It emphasizes the reasoning process, not just the endpoint. Use it in contexts where the method of arriving at an answer matters—detective work, logic puzzles, formal argument.
