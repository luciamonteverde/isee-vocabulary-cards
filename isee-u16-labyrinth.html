What It Means

A labyrinth is a complicated network of winding passages that is easy to get lost in.

Here's how to decide when labyrinth is the exact word.

Worked Example

The castle's interior was such a labyrinth of narrow corridors and hidden chambers that guests relied on printed maps to find their way to the main hall.

Labyrinth emphasizes the confusing, winding nature of the passages and how easy it is to get lost. While 'maze' is similar, a maze is deliberately designed to challenge the solver, whereas a labyrinth suggests an older, organically complex structure. Labyrinth carries more grandeur and historical weight.

Your Turn

The app's menu had three main sections, each with a few buttons.

Would labyrinth describe this interface, or is it too strong?

organized

Correct — Three clear sections with a few buttons each is simply an organized menu; you can see exactly where everything is, so labyrinth is too strong.

labyrinth

Not quite — A labyrinth is a maze of winding, confusing paths where you lose your bearings, and a tidy three-section menu is far too simple for that.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
maze

A network of passages designed to confuse or challenge the solver. Labyrinth can be accidental or natural; a maze is typically designed as a puzzle.

network

An interconnected system of routes or connections. Network lacks the sense of confusion and being lost that labyrinth implies.

tangle

A jumbled, confused mess without clear structure or form. Labyrinth has winding paths and architecture; a tangle is random disorder.

Antonyms · opposites

clarity — The state of being clear and easy to understand. The opposite of the confusion and disorientation a labyrinth creates.

Build the Word

labyrinthus Greek: labyrinthus, from ancient mythology

In ancient Greek legend, the Labyrinth of Crete was a prison designed by Daedalus to trap the Minotaur—a building so complex and winding that no one could escape from it.

The intricate design of the ancient temple impressed all who studied it.

Same root, new word — what does intricate mean here?

plain and simple

Not quite — A design can be simple, but 'impressed all who studied it' suggests something rich and detailed, not plain.

brightly colored

Not quite — Ancient temples can be colorful, but intricate is about how complex and interwoven the design is, not its color.

complex and detailed, with many interwoven parts

Correct — Honesty first: labyrinth and intricate do not share a root. Labyrinth comes from the pre-Greek labyrinthos (a word so old its source is lost), while intricate comes from the Latin tricae, 'tangles.' They are paired only because both describe tangled complexity.

The root connection →

Treat this as a meaning bridge, not a family tree. Labyrinth's root has no surviving English relatives, but intricate's root tricae ('entanglements') genuinely lives on in words like extricate ('to untangle out of') and intrigue. So the tangle idea connects them, not a shared origin.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / literary

Labyrinth describes a genuinely complex, confusing structure—often physical, sometimes figurative. It carries a sense of history and grandeur, suggesting something where you might genuinely get lost. For a simple puzzle game or organized structure, use 'maze' or 'network' instead.
