Dearth

/durth/ — rhymes with birth · noun

Hear It
What It Means

A dearth is a scarcity or lack of something that is needed or expected to be there.

Show it in context

The coach complained about a dearth of tall players on this year's roster.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when dearth is the exact word.

Worked Example

The desert town faced a dearth of clean water, forcing residents to drive forty miles for supplies.

Dearth emphasizes not just lack, but a serious shortage of something essential and expected. It is more precise than the plain word 'shortage' because it conveys the gravity and critical nature of the situation.

Your Turn

The restaurant had only three desserts on the menu instead of the usual ten.

Is dearth the precise word here?

No

Correct — Three desserts instead of ten is just fewer than usual. 'Dearth' signals that something critically needed has run dangerously short, so 'shortage' or 'lack' fits this menu better.

Yes

Not quite — Calling it a dearth overstates things. A dearth is a serious lack of something important, not simply a menu offering fewer choices than normal.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
shortage

Both mean insufficient amount, but dearth emphasizes severity and the critical nature of the lack.

insufficiency

Both focus on falling short, but insufficiency is more technical and neutral about how serious the gap is.

paucity

Both describe scarcity, but paucity feels rare and abstract; dearth is more direct about urgent, felt absence.

Antonyms · opposites

abundance — The state where something is plentiful and available—everything dearth is not.

Dearth—the serious, critical lack of something needed; more urgent than shortage. (our word)

Build the Word

dere Middle English: 'costly, valued'

When something is dear (valuable), its absence is deeply felt—that's what dearth names.

Fresh fruit was so dear that winter that most families bought it only for holidays.

Same root, new word — what does dear mean here?

expensive; costly

Correct — Both words grow from Old English 'deore,' meaning precious or costly. 'Dearth' names the scarcity that makes something costly, and 'dear' can still mean expensive, as it does here.

beloved; treasured

Not quite — 'Dear' does often mean beloved, but here it describes fruit families could barely afford, so this sentence is about price, not affection.

plentiful; easy to get

Not quite — This is the opposite of the clue: if fruit were plentiful, families would not save it only for holidays.

The root connection →

Remember that a 'dearth' of food once meant food had become 'dear,' or pricey, because so little was left. The same 'deore' root ties the shortage to the high price it drives.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / literary

Dearth is formal and literary—reach for it when you need gravity, when discussing something critically needed that is missing. For everyday shortage or insufficient supply, simpler words like 'lack' or 'shortage' are better choices.
