Barren

/BARE-en/ — rhymes with Karen · adjective

Hear It
What It Means

Land that is barren is so dry, empty, or infertile that little or nothing can grow there.

Show it in context

Nothing grew on the barren hillside except a few tufts of dry grass.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when barren is the exact word.

Worked Example

The desert explorer trudged across the barren expanse where the parched earth cracked beneath her feet and nothing could survive.

Barren precisely captures why the land is empty—it's unable to support life due to being both dry and infertile. Just saying 'empty' wouldn't explain the reason; 'barren' shows both the dryness and the lifelessness.

Your Turn

The house had no furniture; each room was bare and quiet.

Is barren the precise word here, or should it just say 'bare'?

bare

Correct — The rooms are just empty of furniture, which 'bare' names exactly. 'Barren' suggests land too dry or poor to grow anything, which does not fit an empty house.

barren

Not quite — 'Barren' usually describes ground that cannot produce life or plants. An unfurnished room is simply 'bare,' meaning uncovered or empty of contents.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
empty

Void of contents; barren specifically describes land unable to grow things due to dryness and infertility.

arid

Very dry; barren includes dryness but also the sense of infertility and complete lifelessness combined.

desolate

Bleak and lonely; barren is specifically about land that won't support life, not about emotional bleakness.

Antonyms · opposites

fertile — Able to produce life and growth abundantly—the opposite of barren's infertility.

How Strong Is It?
bare→barren→fertile→lush

Barren describes land stripped of life and fertility—more severe than bare, less rich than fertile. (our word)

Build the Word

baraigne Old French: unproductive, sterile

Barren originally meant stripped bare and unable to produce—a landscape stripped of the ability to create life.

The hikers reached a bare hilltop where nothing grew but a few dry weeds.

Same root, new word — what does bare mean here?

crowded and overgrown

Not quite — This is the opposite of the clue 'nothing grew but a few dry weeds,' which shows the hilltop is stripped, not lush.

empty and uncovered, with little or nothing on it

Correct — Many etymologists trace 'barren' back through Old French to the same Germanic root that gave 'bare' (Proto-Germanic *bazaz), though the origin is disputed. If that theory holds, both point to emptiness: bare ground and a barren field alike lack cover or life.

steep and rocky

Not quite — 'Bare' is about being uncovered and empty, not about a slope's steepness; the hilltop could be flat and still be bare.

The root connection →

Link them by the idea of nothing there: a 'bare' hilltop has no plants, and a 'barren' field grows none. The shared Germanic root is the leading explanation, even if scholars do not treat it as fully settled.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

neutral

Barren is specific to land or soil that cannot grow things because it is too dry and infertile. Empty just means the space has no contents. Barren emphasizes the inability to produce life.
