Exult

/ɪɡˈzʌlt/ — ig-ZULT, stress on second syllable · verb

Hear It
What It Means

To exult is to feel or show great joy and triumph, especially after winning or succeeding at something.

Show it in context

The fans exulted in the streets after the championship victory.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when exult is the exact word.

Worked Example

When the astronauts' spacecraft touched down safely on Mars, mission control erupted into celebration, and the lead engineer couldn't restrain herself—she leaped from her chair, arms raised, tears streaming down her face.

Exult captures not just happiness but the intense, uninhibited JOY and TRIUMPH of a major achievement. 'Celebrate' or 'rejoice' alone wouldn't convey the sense of overwhelming triumph and the physical expression of it.

Your Turn

After his daughter's violin recital, the father applauded and smiled warmly.

Is exult the right word here?

No

Correct — 'No' fits: exult means unrestrained, triumphant joy you can barely contain, and a warm smile with applause is heartfelt but calm approval, not that overflowing celebration.

Yes

Not quite — Saying 'yes' treats warm applause as full-blown triumph, but the father is simply showing proud approval, not leaping-for-joy elation.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
celebrate

To mark an occasion with joy, but it's more about the event than about the triumphant feeling itself.

rejoice

To feel great joy, but it's quieter and more solemn than exult's jubilant, uninhibited triumph.

triumph

To win decisively, but as a verb it emphasizes the victory itself, not the joyful emotion that exult highlights.

Antonyms · opposites

despair — The complete absence of hope and joy — the opposite extreme of exult's jubilation.

Build the Word

exsultāre Latin: 'to leap out, to jump up' (ex- out + saltāre to leap)

The image is jumping — when you exult, your joy is so great you can barely contain it and want to leap into the air.

The acrobat performed an impressive somersault across the stage.

Same root, new word — what does somersault mean here?

a slow, careful bow to the audience

Not quite — This is tempting because it's a stage move, but the saltare ('leap') root means somersault involves leaping and flipping, not a gentle bow.

a leaping flip that rolls the body over

Correct — Correct. Exult is ex- ('out') plus saltare ('to leap'), joy that makes you jump, and somersault carries that same salt- ('leap') root in its flipping, airborne movement.

a long pause before speaking

Not quite — This is tempting because performers pause dramatically, but salt- means 'leap,' so a somersault is an athletic flip, not a still moment.

The root connection →

Exult and somersault both spring from the Latin saltare, 'to leap': when you exult, joy makes you want to leap up, and a somersault is a literal leaping flip. The salt- root connects the feeling to the physical jump.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / literary

Exult is a grand word for overwhelming joy and triumph — the feeling you can't contain. For everyday happiness, simpler words like 'happy' or 'pleased' fit better. Reach for exult when the joy is powerful and the occasion is significant.
