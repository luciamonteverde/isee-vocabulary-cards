Tirade

/tuh-RAYD/ — stress on second syllable, rhymes with wade · noun

Hear It
What It Means

A tirade is a long, angry speech of criticism or complaint.

Show it in context

The manager launched into a tirade after discovering the shipment had been delayed again.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when tirade is the exact word.

Worked Example

When the restaurant failed to get her order right for the third time, the frustrated customer launched into a fierce tirade, venting months of disappointment and anger about their service.

Tirade captures not just anger but a long, flowing outpouring of criticism — it's a speech, not just a moment of irritation. Just 'complaint' or 'anger' would miss the sense of words tumbling out in a torrent of blame and frustration.

Your Turn

A teacher asked a student to turn in homework on time, and the student said, 'I'll try harder.'

Is tirade the right word here for what the teacher said?

tirade

Not quite — The teacher gave a brief, calm instruction, not the long, angry speech of complaints that a tirade means.

reminder

Correct — A brief, calm instruction to turn in homework on time is simply a reminder, not an angry outburst.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
complaint

An expression of dissatisfaction. Tirade is a long, angry torrent of complaints.

rant

Speaking angrily and intensely about something. Tirade is a more sustained rant, a structured speech of criticism.

harangue

A lengthy speech delivered with force and emotion. Tirade is an angry harangue, specifically one focused on criticism or blame.

Antonyms · opposites

praise — Saying good things. The opposite of tirade — expressing approval instead of angry criticism.

Build the Word

tirade French/Italian: 'tirata' = 'a volley or extended shot'

Tirade comes from a French and Italian root meaning 'to pull out' or 'to draw out.' Like an archer pulling arrows from a quiver and firing them in rapid succession, a tirade is words pulled out and delivered in an angry stream.

The maids helped attire the bride in an elaborate gown, arranging each layer of silk before the ceremony began.

Same root, new word — what does attire mean here?

to scold or criticize someone harshly

Not quite — It is tempting to bounce attire back to the angry 'tirade' and land on scolding, but attire has nothing to do with harsh words. The root shared by these distant cousins is an Old French one meaning 'to draw or pull,' which points to drawing clothing into place, not to criticizing.

to grow weary or exhausted

Not quite — This is a natural mix-up with the look-alike 'tire' (fatigue), but that word comes from a different Old English root. Attire and tirade instead share a root ancestor meaning 'to draw or pull,' and drawing has nothing to do with growing weary.

to dress or clothe someone, especially in fine or formal clothing

Correct — Attire and tirade are historically related through an Old French root meaning 'to draw or pull,' and you matched that pulling idea: to attire someone once meant to draw the garments into place around them. Linguists treat that link as looser than a clean shared root, so think of the two as distant cousins.

The root connection →

Linguists trace both attire and tirade back to an Old French root meaning 'to draw or pull.' A tirade is angry words drawn out and fired off in a stream, while to attire someone is to draw fine garments into place around them. The shared root is real but disputed, so treat the two as distant cousins rather than an obvious pair.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / literary

Tirade is the word when anger builds into a speech — a long, forceful outpouring of criticism or blame. It's more dramatic than 'complaint' and more structured than 'rant.' Reach for it when you want to show that anger has turned into words, rolling out like a torrent.
