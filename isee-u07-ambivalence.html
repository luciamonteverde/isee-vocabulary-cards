Ambivalence

/am-BIV-uh-lens/ — stress on second syllable, like 'divided' · noun

Hear It
What It Means

Ambivalence is the state of having two conflicting feelings or opinions about something at the same time, making it hard to decide.

Show it in context

Her ambivalence about the move showed: she packed her bags, then unpacked them twice.

See How It Works · follow the thinking, then try one

Here's how to decide when ambivalence is the exact word.

Worked Example

He stared at the acceptance letter to his dream school, thrilled by the opportunity yet devastated at leaving his close friends, his face reflecting his complete ambivalence.

Ambivalence is more precise than 'uncertainty' or 'confusion' because it specifically captures having TWO OPPOSITE FEELINGS at once—not just being unsure. The tension between conflicting emotions is exactly what ambivalence names.

Your Turn

When asked if he likes pizza, Marcus shrugs. He's eaten it twice in his life and has no strong feelings either way.

Is ambivalence the right word for Marcus?

Yes

Not quite — Ambivalence requires two opposing feelings pulling equally. Marcus doesn't feel torn; he simply doesn't care either way.

No

Correct — Indifference fits better. Marcus has no strong feelings at all, while ambivalence would mean being pulled equally by two opposing feelings.

Synonyms · words that mean the same — tap one to see how it differs
doubt

Uncertainty about something; ambivalence is specifically having TWO opposite feelings, not just one concern.

conflict

Tension between opposing forces; ambivalence is specifically YOUR internal emotional conflict, not general opposition.

indecision

Being unable to choose; this describes the result of ambivalence but not the underlying feeling itself.

Antonyms · opposites

conviction — A firm, unwavering belief; conviction is single-sided, while ambivalence is torn between two opposites.

Build the Word

ambi- + valens Latin: both + strong/to be worth

Ambi- means both, -valence means to be strong or to pull; together they mean being pulled equally strongly in two directions.

After weeks in the hospital, the convalescent patient was steadily regaining her strength and could walk a little farther each day.

Same root, new word — what does convalescent mean here?

recovering health and strength after an illness

Correct — The root 'valens' means 'strong' or 'to be worth,' the same '-valence' that in ambivalence means being pulled strongly two ways. In convalescent that strength returns: the sentence shows the patient steadily regaining exactly that, her strength, as she recovers.

confined to a hospital bed for many months

Not quite — Confined implies staying put, but the sentence shows the patient's strength and mobility improving each day. The root 'valens' means 'strong,' so the answer should be about regaining strength, not being stuck in bed.

suffering from a serious long-term illness

Not quite — That describes still being sick, but the sentence shows the patient getting stronger, not staying ill. The root 'valens' means 'strong,' pointing toward recovering strength rather than remaining unwell.

The root connection →

Ambivalence combines 'ambi-' (both) with 'valens' (strong), the feeling of being pulled strongly in both directions. A convalescent person carries that same 'valens': someone growing strong again while recovering from an illness.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

formal / psychological

Ambivalence is the precise term when someone is genuinely torn between two opposing feelings or opinions. Use it when the conflict is real and significant enough to affect behavior, like hesitation about a decision. For milder uncertainty, 'doubt' works better; for situations where someone simply doesn't care, use 'indifference' instead.
