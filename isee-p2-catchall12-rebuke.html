Rebuke

rih-BYOOK -- from Old French, literally 'to beat back': a rebuke beats back bad behavior with sharp words. verb

Hear It
What It Means

To rebuke to scold or criticize someone sharply.

Show it in context

The judge paused the trial to rebuke the lawyer for interrupting the witness again.

See How It Works · a worked example, then try one yourself
Worked Example

When a cadet shows up late with a sloppy uniform, the drill sergeant plants himself an inch away and delivers thirty seconds of ice-cold, cutting words that leave the whole line silent.

That sharp, stern scolding is exactly what it means to rebuke someone. Unlike 'nag,' which drips criticism over time, a rebuke lands all at once, hard and direct.

prefixre-back (Latin, via Old French)
root-buketo strike, beat (Old French (buchier, 'to chop, strike'))

Old French rebuker joined re- ('back') with buchier ('to strike, chop') -- to rebuke someone is literally to beat their behavior back with words. The striking root -buke survives only in this word, though the re- prefix also appears in rebuff, another word for pushing someone back.

Use the root in a new word: rebuff

The senator's aides expected a warm welcome but met a cold rebuff at the embassy.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

Rebuke usually takes a person as its object -- you rebuke someone for something. Some word lists tag rebuke as a noun (as in 'a sharp rebuke'), but it is taught here as the verb, 'to scold sharply.'
