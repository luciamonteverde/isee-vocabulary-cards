Infer

in-FUR -- two syllables, stress on the second syllable, FUR. verb

Hear It
What It Means

To infer to deduce something by evidence or reason.

Show it in context

From the empty parking lot, we could infer that the game had been canceled.

See How It Works · a worked example, then try one yourself
Worked Example

The teacher never announces the test. But the review sheet runs three pages, the seating chart has been redrawn, and she has asked twice whether anyone will be absent on Thursday.

From those clues a student can infer that Thursday is the test -- a conclusion reached by reasoning from evidence. Notice who does which job: the teacher's actions imply, and the student infers. The one receiving the clues is always the one who infers.

prefixin-in, into (Latin)
rootfer-to carry, to bring (Latin ferre)

Infer comes from Latin inferre -- in- ('in') plus ferre ('to carry') -- 'to carry in.' When you infer, you carry a conclusion INTO your own mind from the evidence in front of you. That direction is the whole difference from imply, where a speaker sends a hint out.

Word Choice · its tone, and when to use it

One rule matters most: the listener or reader INFERS; the speaker or writer IMPLIES. If a coach says 'some of you did not run today,' he is implying that some players slacked off, and the players infer it. Never write 'his tone inferred anger' -- a tone cannot infer, because inferring is something a mind does with evidence.
